A deviated septum means the wall between your two nostrils sits off-center. Many crooked septums cause no trouble. When one does, the main problem is a blocked nose, and the fix is a surgery called septoplasty.

## What is a deviated septum?

A deviated septum is a septum (the wall of cartilage and thin bone that divides the nose into two sides) that bends or tilts to one side. That can narrow one nasal passage and make air flow harder.

A perfectly straight septum is uncommon, so a little curve is not a disease. The question is whether the curve causes symptoms. The American Academy of Otolaryngology (ENT Health) describes it the same way: it matters when it blocks breathing or contributes to other nose problems.

## What are the symptoms of a deviated septum?

The most common symptom is a blocked nose, often worse on one side. Other signs can include nosebleeds, a dry nose on the blocked side, noisy breathing, and trouble sleeping on one side.

Here is what people commonly report:

- One nostril that feels stuffy most of the time, not just during a cold
- Trouble breathing through the nose during exercise
- Dry mouth or snoring from mouth breathing at night
- Nosebleeds or crusting inside the nose
- Facial pressure or repeated sinus infections in some people

Not every symptom on that list comes from the septum. Allergies, swollen nasal lining, and the normal nasal cycle can all block a nostril. The nasal cycle is the natural switching of airflow from side to side, explained in [Why One Nostril Is Always Blocked: The Nasal Cycle Explained](https://healthynoses.com/nasal-cycle-one-nostril-blocked/). A blockage that never switches sides is more likely to be structural.

## Does a deviated septum cause sinus infections?

A crooked septum can narrow the nasal passages, and for some people that may contribute to poor drainage. It is not the only cause, and it is not proven to be the reason for every repeat infection.

Chronic sinusitis has many drivers, including allergies, polyps and swollen lining. A doctor usually checks the inside of the nose with a small scope, and sometimes a CT scan, to see what is going on. Septoplasty is also sometimes done during endoscopic sinus surgery. According to a StatPearls review, it can be performed as part of functional endoscopic sinus surgery to improve surgical exposure and access, not only to fix breathing.

## What is septoplasty, and how is it different from rhinoplasty?

Septoplasty is surgery that straightens or repositions the septum to improve airflow. Rhinoplasty changes the outside shape of the nose. The two can be done together, but they have different goals.

The StatPearls review calls septoplasty a functional operation, meaning its main purpose is to fix breathing, not looks. It notes that the usual reason for surgery is a deviated septum causing significant, symptomatic nasal blockage. Surgeons can work through the nostrils, with an endoscope, or through an open approach. Septoplasty may also be paired with turbinate surgery or rhinoplasty.

| Procedure | Main goal | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| Septoplasty | Better airflow | A crooked septum |
| Rhinoplasty | Change nose shape (can also help breathing) | Outer nose structure |
| Turbinate reduction | Better airflow | Enlarged turbinates (ridges inside the nose) |
| Sinus surgery or balloon dilation | Open sinus drainage pathways | Blocked sinus openings |

## Is the septum always the problem?

No. The inferior turbinates, the ridges of tissue along the sides of the nose, are often a major cause of blockage. A 2013 paper in Aesthetic Surgery Journal by plastic surgeons calls them a principal cause of nasal airway obstruction, and says the bony septum only occasionally contributes.

That paper is a description of an operative technique, not a trial. The authors propose gently widening the nasal space with a speculum or clamp, and they say this makes further turbinate removal seldom necessary. Those are the authors' own claims. The abstract does not report a comparison with other methods, so it cannot show that this approach is better. The takeaway for patients is simpler: a good exam should check the turbinates as well as the septum, because fixing the wrong structure will not fix the blockage.

## Does septoplasty actually work?

For the right patient, the evidence says yes. A 2024 commentary in Rhinology notes that randomized controlled trials published in 2019 and 2023 both found that patients who had septoplasty did better than control patients.

That commentary adds a caution. Insurers, policymakers and some providers had questioned the value of septoplasty, which is why those trials were done. The commentary authors do not dispute the results. They say septoplasty is of great value to the right patient. But they urge care when applying trial results to everyday practice. They push back on the idea that the results apply to every patient with nasal blockage from a deviated septum. This is a short opinion piece, so it does not give new numbers. The main point is that patient selection matters.

The StatPearls review agrees, stating that appropriate patient selection is crucial to good outcomes.

### What about complex or severe cases?

A 2024 Brazilian study looked at a specialized method called modified extracorporeal septoplasty. The surgeon removes the septum, reshapes it outside the body, and puts it back.

The study followed 27 patients at one hospital, using a validated nasal obstruction questionnaire called NOSE-p. Scores fell from an average of 65.2 before surgery to 23.5 after surgery. Lower is better. Residual septal deviation remained in 2 patients (7.4%). The authors reported a low rate of complications.

There are limits. It was a single-center study with no control group, and it is rated Level 3 evidence. It involved a technique for complex cases, so it does not describe a typical septoplasty.

## How long is septoplasty recovery?

Recovery usually lasts a few weeks, and serious complications are rare, according to StatPearls. The first week is usually the roughest, with stuffiness, mild drainage, and sometimes soft splints inside the nose.

Exact rules vary by surgeon, so follow the written instructions you receive. These are common guidelines:

- Expect a blocked, stuffy nose at first. Swelling goes down gradually.
- Avoid blowing your nose until your surgeon says it is safe.
- Skip heavy lifting and hard workouts for the period your surgeon sets.
- Sleep with your head raised if told to.
- Light bleeding or oozing can happen. For a steady nosebleed, sit up, lean forward, and pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 minutes.

The Jersey Shore's dry winter indoor heat and damp summer air can both affect how a healing nose feels. Dry air may bring more crusting, and humid air may make swelling feel worse. Your surgical team can tell you what rinses or sprays are safe during healing.

## When should you see a doctor about a deviated septum?

See a doctor if a blocked nose lasts more than a few weeks, keeps coming back, or disrupts sleep or exercise. Get care sooner for the warning signs below.

- Nosebleeds that do not stop after 20 minutes of steady pressure
- Sinus infections that return often, or symptoms lasting more than 12 weeks
- A nose that becomes crooked or blocked after an injury
- Fever, swelling around the eyes, severe headache, or vision changes
- After surgery: heavy bleeding, fever, or worsening pain

Call emergency services for vision changes, swelling around the eye, confusion, or a stiff neck with fever. For a long-running blockage, a specialist can examine the nose, look at the turbinates and the septum, and say whether surgery makes sense.
