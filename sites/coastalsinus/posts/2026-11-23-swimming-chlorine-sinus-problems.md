Many people notice a stuffy, itchy or drippy nose after a swim. Chlorine is the usual suspect, and research gives it some support. The evidence is mostly from competitive swimmers, though, so it does not map neatly onto a weekend lap swimmer or a family beach day.

## Why does my nose get stuffy or runny after swimming?

Pool chemicals can irritate the lining of the nose. Chlorine reacts with sweat, skin cells and urine in the water and forms by-products called chloramines, trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids. A 2018 overview in the Annals of Agricultural and Environmental Medicine says these compounds are most concentrated just above the water surface. They can irritate the skin, eyes and the lining of the airways.

That matters for swimmers because the face is right at that layer. Every breath near the surface pulls in whatever is floating above the water. The nose reacts with swelling, mucus and sneezing. This is irritation, not necessarily an infection.

Other causes also exist: water forced up the nose, cold water, and plain exercise. Running and other hard exercise can also make the nose react. That is why researchers compared swimmers with runners.

## What do studies say about chlorine and nasal symptoms?

The research points to a link between heavy pool exposure and nasal symptoms. It is not strong enough to say how much chlorine causes how much trouble. Here is what each source found.

| Source | Design | Main finding | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alves et al., 2010 (PMID 21244727) | 19 international-level swimmers and 13 professional runners measured before and after a training session | Exercise-induced rhinitis (a drop in nasal airflow above 20%) in 21% of swimmers and 23% of runners. Swimmers had more sneezing, congestion, itching and postnasal drip after training; runners did not. | Very small. Only postnasal drip differed significantly between groups (p = 0.050). Elite athletes only. |
| Bougault and Boulet, 2012 (PMID 22247299) | Review of airway problems in competitive swimmers | Rhinitis, airway over-reactivity and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction are highly common in elite swimmers who train in chlorinated indoor pools. | Effect on performance is unclear. Elite swimmers only. |
| Kanikowska et al., 2018 (PMID 30586974) | Overview of chlorinated water and allergic disease | Swimming in chlorinated water may be linked to asthma-like symptoms and rhinitis, especially in children, elite swimmers and indoor pool workers. | Effects of low chlorine levels are not fully known. |
| Bougault et al., 2022 (PMID 35365432) | Systematic review: 7 papers, 1,567 athletes | Pool chloramine levels were identified as a risk factor for rhinitis and breathing symptoms during exercise. | Only a handful of papers. Only one addressed rhinitis from chlorinated pools. |
| Boraczyński et al., 2025 (PMID 39835195) | Narrative review on disinfection by-products and lung function | Regular and competitive swimmers face higher risk of respiratory problems. Occasional or low-level exposure might not be harmful. Symptoms may be reversible when training volume drops. | Narrative review, not a pooled analysis. Focused mostly on the lungs. |

The main takeaway: the signal is clearest in people who swim a lot, indoors, for years.

## Does chlorine cause sinus infections?

No study here shows that chlorine causes sinus infections. What the research shows is irritation of the nasal lining, which looks like a cold or allergies. A swollen, irritated nose can feel blocked and can make sinus drainage worse, but irritation is not the same as infection.

A sinus infection usually looks different. Thick colored mucus, facial pressure and a blocked nose that last more than 10 days point more toward infection. Our guide on [how long a sinus infection lasts](/how-long-does-a-sinus-infection-last/) covers the timeline in detail.

If a stuffy nose shows up soon after swimming and fades within hours, irritation is the more likely explanation. If it lingers for days with pain or fever, think infection.

## Is it swimmer's rhinitis or allergies?

It may be neither. In the Alves study, every swimmer with exercise-induced rhinitis tested negative for allergy on skin-prick testing. The authors said this supports a "swimming-induced rhinitis" that does not depend on allergy status.

That fits a nonallergic pattern. If you want to understand that category, see [why a stuffy nose can happen with no cold](https://healthynoses.com/stuffy-nose-no-cold-nonallergic-rhinitis/).

The same study also found that allergic athletes tended to feel better after exercise. The difference was not statistically significant, so it should not be treated as a rule.

## Who is most at risk?

The research names a few groups:

- Elite and heavy-training swimmers, who spend many hours a week breathing pool air
- Children who swim often
- Indoor pool workers, such as lifeguards and instructors

The 2025 review says occasional or low-level exposure might not be harmful. A few splashes at a summer pool are very different from two-a-day indoor training. Note that the evidence in recreational swimmers is limited.

## How can I reduce nose irritation from pools?

The 2025 review lists practical steps for the pool itself: less chlorine use, good hygiene before swimming, swim caps and better ventilation. Some of these are in the pool operator's hands. Some are in yours.

### Things swimmers can control

- **Shower before you get in.** Sweat, skin cells and body oils react with chlorine and make by-products. Showering first gives chlorine less to react with.
- **Pick a well-ventilated pool.** If the air smells sharply of chemicals, that is a sign by-products are building up. Think about swimming somewhere else or at another time.
- **Use a swim cap.** The review lists caps as one step to reduce risk. Hair also carries a lot of material into the water.
- **Try a nose clip.** It keeps water out of the nose. No study here tested clips for nasal symptoms, so this is a comfort measure.
- **Cut back if symptoms are steady.** In heavy swimmers, reviewers note that bronchial symptoms tend to ease when training volume and intensity go down.

### After you get out

A saline rinse can wash away irritants. Only use distilled, sterile or previously boiled and cooled water. Never use plain tap water in a rinse. The FDA warns about the risks of untreated tap water in nasal rinsing. Our guide on [rinse bottle vs neti pot safety](/sinus-rinse-bottle-vs-neti-pot/) shows the right method.

## What about ocean, bay and lake swimming at the Shore?

Ocean water has no chlorine by-products, so it avoids that specific irritant. Bay and lake water have their own issues, such as water quality after heavy rain. If seawater stings or you feel congested afterward, a rinse with safe water helps. For more on ocean air and seawater, see [Salt Air and Seawater: Does the Ocean Help Your Sinuses?](/salt-air-seawater-sinuses/).

If you swim at an indoor pool through winter, the chlorine pattern above applies more.

## Can I swim with a sinus infection?

Often yes, if you feel well enough and have no fever. Swimming does not make a viral cold worse. Still, a few cautions make sense:

- Skip diving or deep dives if you have facial pain or pressure. Pressure changes can hurt with blocked sinuses.
- Skip swimming if you have a fever or feel unwell.
- Chlorine may irritate an already inflamed nose, so a short, easy swim is a better test than hard laps.
- If water in the nose makes pain worse, get out and rest.

## When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor if symptoms last or are severe. Get checked for:

- Nasal blockage or drip lasting more than 10 days without improvement, or more than 12 weeks in any case
- Fever, or symptoms that get better and then suddenly worsen
- Facial pain, swelling around the eyes, or a severe headache
- Wheezing, chest tightness or cough during or after swimming, which can point to airway problems beyond the nose
- Nosebleeds that keep returning
- Symptoms that appear every time you swim and do not go away with the steps above

Get urgent care for vision changes, a stiff neck, confusion or swelling and redness around an eye.

If pool symptoms keep coming back, a nose and sinus exam can sort out irritation, allergy and structural causes, and readers near the coast can find [sinus specialists at the Jersey Shore](/jersey-shore-sinus-doctor/) who see swimmers and beachgoers.
