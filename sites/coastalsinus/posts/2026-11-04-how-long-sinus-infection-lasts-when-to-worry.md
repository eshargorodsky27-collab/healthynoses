Most sinus infections get better on their own within about 7 to 10 days. Some last longer, and a few need medical care. This guide explains the normal timeline, the signs that point to a bacterial infection, and the red flags that mean it is time to call a doctor.

## How long does a sinus infection last?

Most episodes of acute sinusitis are self-limited and last 7 to 10 days, according to a 2025 rapid evidence review in American Family Physician. Acute sinusitis can last up to 4 weeks, but a typical cold-triggered case is much shorter.

Doctors use the length of symptoms to sort sinus problems into groups. These labels come from a StatPearls clinical reference.

| Type | How long symptoms last |
|---|---|
| Acute | Fewer than 4 weeks |
| Subacute | 4 to 12 weeks |
| Chronic | More than 12 weeks |
| Recurrent acute | 4 or more episodes a year, each lasting at least 10 days, with symptoms clearing in between |

The number of days matters. A stuffy nose on day 3 is very different from the same stuffy nose on day 30.

## Why do most sinus infections go away without antibiotics?

Most start as a virus. A 2020 review in Current Allergy and Asthma Reports describes acute sinusitis as mainly an inflammatory disease triggered by a viral infection, with few cases developing into a bacterial infection. In most cases it resolves on its own, and treatment is aimed at symptoms.

The same review reports that complications are uncommon and do not depend on whether antibiotics were used. It also notes that antibiotics should be limited to cases where a bacterial infection is clearly suspected.

This matters because sinusitis is common. The American Family Physician review says acute sinusitis sends more than 30 million people in the United States to seek care each year. It also says respiratory infections, including bronchitis and sinusitis, account for 75% of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions in primary care. Antibiotics do nothing against a virus.

## How can you tell a viral sinus infection from a bacterial one?

It is hard, even for doctors. Both types cause congestion, pressure and discolored mucus. The 2020 review says the two have similar symptoms, though a combination of specific signs can tell them apart.

The American Family Physician review lists the classic features of acute bacterial sinusitis:

- Facial pain or pressure on one side
- Fever above 102°F (39°C)
- Thick, pus-like nasal discharge together with a blocked nose

The same review says no simple test settles it. Sampling the sinus with a needle is too invasive to be practical. A blood test for C-reactive protein (a marker of inflammation) may help, but it is not widely available. So the diagnosis usually rests on symptoms and timing.

Color alone is not a reliable clue. Yellow or green mucus appears in many ordinary colds.

## When do doctors consider antibiotics?

The American Family Physician review says antibiotics should be considered in three situations:

1. Severe symptoms for 3 or more days
2. Significant worsening after 3 to 5 days of symptoms
3. Symptoms lasting 7 or more days

The word is "considered," not "required." The doctor weighs how sick you are, your health history and any allergies. The review notes that amoxicillin worked as well as amoxicillin-clavulanate as a first choice for people without a penicillin-type allergy. For people with that allergy, other antibiotics are options.

Only a clinician can decide this. Leftover pills from an old prescription are not a safe plan.

## What helps while waiting it out?

Supportive care is the main plan for most people. The American Family Physician review says saline irrigation, nasal steroid sprays, antihistamines and decongestants may help reduce symptom severity. The review does not rank them or promise a faster recovery.

Practical steps that fit this guidance:

- Rinse with saline, using distilled, boiled-and-cooled or sterile water. The FDA warns against tap water in nasal rinse devices. For more on rinsing, see [what the studies show about nasal irrigation](https://healthynoses.com/nasal-irrigation-sinus-symptoms-studies/).
- Drink fluids and rest.
- Use pain relievers as directed on the label for facial pressure and headache.
- Ask a pharmacist before using decongestant sprays, since long use can backfire.

Rinses, sprays and air conditioning can leave the inside of the nose dry and sore. Along the Jersey Shore, summer air conditioning and dry winter heating both do this. Some people add a nasal moisturizer such as Narisure, which soothes and moisturizes dry nasal passages. It is for comfort only and does not shorten an infection.

## Do sinus infections last longer in children?

Not necessarily. A 2025 review in Paediatric Respiratory Reviews says acute sinusitis in children usually follows a viral cold. The viral form is self-limiting, so it clears without antibiotics.

The review also says it is important to separate a simple cold, a post-viral sinus infection and a true bacterial infection. That is because bacterial sinusitis in children can lead to severe complications involving the eye area or the brain. It says saline rinses, corticosteroids and pain relievers may provide symptom relief, and that antibiotics improved symptom scores in bacterial cases.

If a child seems very sick, has swelling around an eye, or a cold that is getting worse after a few days, call a pediatrician rather than waiting.

## What if it keeps coming back or will not go away?

There are two patterns to watch.

**Symptoms past 12 weeks.** This is the cutoff for chronic sinusitis, per the StatPearls reference. Two or more of these signs are typical: blocked nose, discolored drainage, facial pressure, or a reduced sense of smell.

**Four or more infections in a year.** The StatPearls reference calls this recurrent acute sinusitis when each episode lasts at least 10 days and symptoms clear in between.

The American Family Physician review says a CT scan and a visit to an ear, nose and throat specialist (otolaryngologist) should be considered for repeated sinus infections or any concern about complications. Allergies, a crooked septum or polyps can all play a part, and a specialist can look for them.

These sources are reviews and clinical references. They summarize earlier research. They do not report new trials, and individual recovery can differ.

## When should you see a doctor about a sinus infection?

Book an appointment if:

- Symptoms last 7 or more days with no improvement
- You felt better, then got clearly worse after 3 to 5 days
- Symptoms are severe for 3 or more days, including a fever above 102°F (39°C)
- Pain or pressure is strong and mostly on one side of the face
- You have had four or more sinus infections in a year
- Symptoms have lasted more than 12 weeks

Get urgent care, or call 911, if you have:

- Swelling, redness or pain around an eye
- Double vision or any change in vision
- A severe headache with a stiff neck
- Confusion, unusual sleepiness or a seizure
- A swollen, puffy forehead

These can signal a rare complication. The American Family Physician review lists orbital cellulitis (infection around the eye), meningitis and abscess. They are uncommon, but they need fast treatment.
