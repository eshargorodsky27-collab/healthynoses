Balloon dilation and endoscopic sinus surgery both aim to open blocked sinus drainage pathways. Many people want to know which one works better. The research gives a more cautious answer than most marketing does.

This digest looks at three studies: a meta-analysis that compared the two procedures head to head, a randomized trial of balloon dilation in people with repeated sinus infections, and a large look at how both procedures are used in children.

## Is balloon sinus dilation as effective as endoscopic sinus surgery?

The best available pooled data suggest the two procedures give similar symptom changes, but the authors of that analysis said the evidence is limited. That is not the same as proof that they are equal.

### How the meta-analysis was done

A 2023 paper in the *Annals of Otology, Rhinology & Laryngology* (Sinha and colleagues, from Washington University and the University of Virginia) pooled earlier research on chronic rhinosinusitis. A medical librarian ran the search. Two reviewers separately decided which studies to include.

The team reviewed 315 abstracts. They kept 18 studies for a qualitative review. Seven went into the numerical meta-analysis: 4 randomized clinical trials and 3 cohort studies. Each compared how much patients' scores changed on the SNOT-20, a patient-filled symptom questionnaire, after balloon dilation versus functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS).

### What it found

Only 3 of those studies (2 trials and 1 cohort) reported the data needed for the main calculation. For those, the pooled difference in means between the procedures was 0.435. The authors compared this with a clinically meaningful difference of 0.8. The result fell below that bar.

In a second, sensitivity analysis, the team added 4 more studies. They had to estimate (impute) some missing statistics. Assuming a high correlation (0.8) between before and after scores, the pooled difference was 0.237. It was again below 0.8.

### What it cannot tell us

- The authors themselves concluded there is limited high-quality evidence comparing the two procedures.
- The main analysis rested on only 3 studies. The extra analysis relied on imputed numbers.
- Cohort studies, unlike randomized trials, can differ in who got which procedure.
- The abstract does not report long-term results, complications or revision surgery rates, so this review cannot answer those questions.

The authors called for future studies that compare balloon dilation with endoscopic surgery, hybrid procedures and medical care alone, using validated objective and patient-reported measures.

## Does balloon sinus dilation help people with repeated sinus infections?

One small randomized trial found that adding in-office balloon dilation to medical care improved quality of life more than medical care alone at 24 weeks. The trial was small, and the result was only just statistically significant.

### How the trial was done

Sikand and colleagues published this study in *International Forum of Allergy & Rhinology* in 2019. It enrolled adults with recurrent acute rhinosinusitis (RARS). That means repeated short bouts of sinus infection, not constant symptoms.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups:

- Balloon dilation in the office plus medical management (29 people)
- Medical management alone (30 people)

The medical-management-only group also got a sham balloon procedure. This kept everyone blind to which group they were in. Follow-up ran to 48 weeks. The main outcome was change in Chronic Sinusitis Survey (CSS) score from baseline to 24 weeks.

### What it found

At 24 weeks, the CSS total score improved by 37.3 points (± 24.4, n = 26) in the balloon group and by 21.8 points (± 29.0, n = 27) in the medical-only group. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0424). The authors concluded that balloon dilation plus medical care was superior to medical care alone for quality of life in properly diagnosed RARS patients.

### What it cannot tell us

- The trial was small. Only 53 people had 24-week scores in the analysis.
- A p-value of 0.0424 is close to the usual 0.05 cutoff. A small shift in the data could change that.
- The scores were self-reported. Sham-controlled design reduces bias, but the spread of scores was wide.
- It did not compare balloon dilation with endoscopic surgery.
- It covered adults with recurrent acute sinusitis, not people with long-lasting chronic sinusitis.

## How often is balloon sinuplasty used in children?

Very rarely, compared with traditional surgery. A national database study found balloon procedures made up under 4% of pediatric sinus operations, and the authors said its role in children is unclear.

### How the study was done

Thong and colleagues, from Boston Children's Hospital and Harvard, published a retrospective review in *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery* in 2019. They used a national pediatric database. They studied children aged 18 or younger who had balloon sinuplasty or traditional endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). The window was 5 years before and after balloon billing codes were introduced.

### What it found

| Measure | Balloon sinuplasty | Traditional ESS |
|---|---|---|
| Patients (of 14,079 total) | 524 | 13,555 |
| Median age | 6 years | 9 years |
| 30-day readmissions | 23 (4.4%) | 474 (3.5%) |
| Median cost, maxillary antrostomy | $6,560 | $5,630 |

Balloon use did not significantly increase between 2011 and 2016. The authors reported no difference in readmission rates. Balloon procedures cost more. Surgeons who did balloon procedures also did more traditional ESS than those who did not.

### What it cannot tell us

This was a review of billing and hospital records. It did not measure whether children felt better, how sinus problems changed or whether anyone needed repeat surgery. It shows how procedures were used, not how well they worked. The authors said the role of balloon sinuplasty in children with chronic sinusitis remains unclear.

## What does this evidence mean for someone deciding on a procedure?

The evidence does not show one procedure is clearly better. It also does not show they are identical. The studies leave real gaps.

- **Balloon dilation looks roughly comparable to FESS on symptom scores in pooled data,** but that rests on few studies.
- **For repeated acute infections, one small trial favored balloon dilation plus medical care over medical care alone.** It needs larger replication.
- **Direct comparisons are scarce.** Questions about long-term durability and revision rates are not answered in these studies.
- **Children are a special case.** Most pediatric sinus surgery in the database was traditional ESS.

Good questions to bring to a specialist:

1. Which of my sinus openings are blocked, and does a scan show it?
2. Have I had a full trial of medical care first?
3. Why do you recommend this procedure over the other one for my anatomy?
4. What are the chances I will need another procedure?
5. Is a deviated septum or polyps part of my problem? Balloon dilation does not address those.

## When should you see a doctor about sinus symptoms?

See a doctor if sinus symptoms last more than 12 weeks, or if infections keep coming back. Do this before anyone discusses a procedure. A proper diagnosis comes first.

Get urgent care for any of these:

- Swelling, redness or pain around an eye
- Vision changes or double vision
- Severe headache with a stiff neck
- A high fever that does not settle
- Confusion or unusual sleepiness

These can signal a complication that needs fast attention.
