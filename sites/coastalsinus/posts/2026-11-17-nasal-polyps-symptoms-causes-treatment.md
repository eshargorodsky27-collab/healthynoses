Nasal polyps are soft, teardrop-shaped growths that form inside the nose and sinuses. They are not cancer, but they can block airflow and dull the sense of smell. Most people with polyps have long-term inflammation of the sinus lining, which doctors call chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP).

## What are nasal polyps?

Nasal polyps are swollen, fluid-filled bits of tissue that hang from the lining of the nose or sinuses. They look a bit like peeled grapes. They are painless to touch because that tissue has few pain nerves.

Small polyps may cause no symptoms. Larger ones, or clusters, can fill the nasal passages and block the drainage openings of the sinuses. Polyps usually appear on both sides of the nose. A polyp on only one side is a reason to get checked sooner.

## What are the symptoms of nasal polyps?

The main symptoms are a stuffy or blocked nose and a weaker sense of smell. These last for weeks or months, not days like a cold.

Common signs include:

- Nasal blockage that does not clear up
- Loss or reduction of smell, and food tasting bland
- A runny nose or mucus dripping down the back of the throat
- Pressure or fullness in the face or forehead
- Snoring or mouth breathing
- Reduced sleep quality

Polyps often come with chronic sinusitis, which is typically defined by symptoms lasting 12 weeks or longer. Smell loss is a clue that points toward polyps rather than a simple cold. The trial summaries below list nasal blockage, loss of smell, facial pressure and lower quality of life as the main burdens of the disease.

## What causes nasal polyps?

No single cause explains every case. Polyps grow from long-lasting inflammation in the nasal lining. In many people this is "type 2" inflammation, an immune pattern that also shows up in allergic asthma. Several of the 2025 trials below describe CRSwNP as predominantly driven by it.

Conditions linked with nasal polyps include:

- Asthma, especially adult-onset asthma
- Allergies, though many people with polyps have no clear allergy
- Sensitivity to aspirin and similar pain relievers (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease)
- Cystic fibrosis, mainly in children and teens
- Repeated or long-lasting sinus infections

Polyps are most often found in adults. Having them once does not mean something you did caused them. Researchers are still sorting out why the immune system drives this inflammation in some people and not others.

## How are nasal polyps diagnosed?

An ear, nose and throat (ENT) doctor usually confirms polyps by looking inside the nose with a thin camera called an endoscope. The visit takes a few minutes and is done in the office.

A CT scan of the sinuses may be ordered to see how far the swelling goes. Doctors often grade polyps on a 0 to 8 scale (0 to 4 per side). In the EVEREST trial, adults needed a score of 5 or more, with at least 2 in each nostril, to count as severe. A doctor may also check for asthma and allergies, since they often travel with polyps.

## How are nasal polyps treated?

Treatment aims to shrink the swelling, open the airways and restore smell. Most people start with sprays and move up in steps only if needed.

### Steroid nasal sprays and saline rinses

A daily steroid nasal spray is the usual first step. It calms the inflammation that feeds polyps. Technique matters, so see [how to use a nasal steroid spray the right way](https://njsinusallergy.com/nasal-steroid-spray-how-to-use/). Saline rinses can wash out mucus and are often used alongside the spray. Use distilled, boiled-then-cooled or properly filtered water, never plain tap water.

### Short courses of oral steroids

For a bad flare, a doctor may prescribe a short course of steroid pills. They can shrink polyps fast. Repeated courses carry side effects, so doctors try to limit them.

### Endoscopic sinus surgery

If medicine does not work, surgery can remove polyps and open the sinus drainage paths. It is done through the nostrils with no outside cuts. Polyps can grow back, because surgery removes the growths but not the inflammation behind them. The WAYPOINT review below notes that CRSwNP carries high recurrence rates.

### Biologic medicines

Biologics are injected drugs that block specific immune signals. They are used for severe polyps that keep returning despite sprays and surgery. Examples named in the 2025 research include dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab, tezepelumab and depemokimab. They are prescribed by specialists and are not for mild disease.

## What do the newest biologic studies show?

Biologics shrink polyps and improve symptoms in trials, but most of the evidence comes from studies of severe disease. Here is what each 2025 paper covered.

| Study | Design | Main point |
|---|---|---|
| Real-world meta-analysis (Allergy, 2025) | 64 real-world studies, 3,921 patients, four biologics | Significant improvements in polyp score and quality of life at most time points. Dupilumab stood out. Few people stopped because of side effects. |
| WAYPOINT (NEJM, 2025) | Phase 3 trial of tezepelumab in severe CRSwNP | A 2025 review of it reports polyp score down 2.07 points and SNOT-22 down 28.4 points. |
| EVEREST (Lancet Respir Med, 2025) | Head-to-head phase 4 trial of dupilumab vs omalizumab in adults with severe CRSwNP and asthma, 100 sites in 17 countries | The first direct comparison of these two biologics. |
| ANCHOR-1 and ANCHOR-2 (Lancet, 2025) | Two phase 3 placebo-controlled trials of depemokimab at 190 centres in 16 countries | Tests a twice-a-year dose in adults with inadequately controlled CRSwNP. |

A few details are worth knowing.

The real-world meta-analysis found that results in everyday practice looked better than in the phase 3 trials. The authors warn that real-world studies have limits. They are not randomized and are less tightly controlled. They call for longer, higher-quality studies.

The tezepelumab review reports that, versus placebo, the WAYPOINT trial cut the need for endoscopic sinus surgery by 98% and the need for oral steroids by 88%. It also reports that nasal congestion scores fell by 1.03 points. These are relative drops in need, not a promise for any one person. At the time of that review, tezepelumab was not approved for CRSwNP and was described as investigational. Several authors of the WAYPOINT paper work for the drug makers.

EVEREST was built to answer a practical question: which of two biologics works better for people who have both polyps and asthma. ANCHOR-1 and ANCHOR-2 look at whether a drug that lasts longer in the body can cut the number of injections to two a year.

These drugs act on the immune system and are costly, so doctors weigh benefits, side effects and insurance rules. They are not a first step.

## Can shore weather and mold make polyps worse?

Damp air, mold and pollen cannot be named as a cause of polyps, but they can make nasal symptoms feel worse. If allergies are part of the picture, summer humidity and mold spores at the shore may add to congestion and drip. Salt air does not cause polyps.

Keeping a log of when symptoms spike can help a specialist spot patterns. Rinsing with safe water and keeping indoor spaces dry may ease day-to-day stuffiness. None of this shrinks an established polyp.

## When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if nasal blockage or loss of smell lasts more than 12 weeks, or if sprays from the drugstore are not helping. Polyps are easier to manage when they are found early.

Get checked promptly for:

- A blocked nose or polyp on one side only
- Nosebleeds that keep coming back
- Loss of smell that came on suddenly
- Snoring with pauses in breathing at night
- Symptoms that return soon after surgery

Go to urgent care or the emergency room for swelling or redness around an eye, double vision or changes in sight, a severe headache with fever, a stiff neck, or confusion. These can signal a spreading infection.
