An aching upper tooth with a stuffy nose and a heavy face leaves most people asking the same question: is it the sinus or the tooth? The two are close neighbors, and each can cause pain that feels like the other. A few simple clues can point to the likely source, and some signs mean it is time to get checked.

## Can a sinus infection really cause tooth pain?

Yes. Sinus inflammation can make the upper teeth ache, especially the back ones. The maxillary sinus, the large sinus in the cheekbone, is closely related to the roots of the upper back teeth ([Whyte and Boeddinghaus, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31386556/)).

The ache is often dull and spread out. It may feel like pressure rather than a sharp, single-tooth jab. It often shows up with other sinus signs, such as congestion, thick nasal drainage and pressure in the cheek.

## Why do the upper teeth hurt when a sinus is inflamed?

The floor of the maxillary sinus sits just above the roots of the upper back teeth. Nerve branches in the same area supply both the teeth and the tissues around the sinus. When the sinus lining swells and pressure builds, the brain can read that signal as tooth pain.

A review of maxillary sinus anatomy describes how closely the sinus relates to the upper teeth and to branches of the trigeminal nerve ([Whyte and Boeddinghaus, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31386556/)). This is why sinus pain tends to show up in the upper teeth and not the lower ones. Lower tooth pain is much less likely to come from a sinus.

## How can you tell sinus tooth pain from a dental problem?

Look at the pattern. Sinus-related pain usually involves several upper teeth and comes with nasal symptoms. Dental pain usually points to one tooth and reacts to things you do with your mouth. These are general patterns, not a diagnosis.

| Clue | More likely sinus | More likely dental |
|---|---|---|
| Which teeth | Several upper back teeth, often on one or both sides | One specific tooth |
| Pain feel | Dull pressure or ache | Sharp, throbbing, or zinging |
| Triggers | Bending over, lying down, changes in head position | Biting, chewing, hot or cold foods |
| Other symptoms | Stuffy nose, thick drainage, cheek pressure, recent cold | Swollen or tender gum, bad taste, a visible crack or cavity |
| Lower teeth | Rarely affected | Can be affected |
| Timing | Often follows a cold or allergy flare | Can come on with no cold at all |

A simple home check: tap gently on the ssore tooth and bite down on something soft. Pain that is sharp in one tooth leans toward a dental cause. An ache across several teeth with a blocked nose leans toward the sinus. Neither test is conclusive.

## What is odontogenic sinusitis, and why does it matter?

Odontogenic sinusitis is a sinus infection that starts from a tooth problem or follows a dental procedure. It is distinct from the more common sinus infections that begin in the nose. A review in the World Journal of Otorhinolaryngology reported that it is more common than historically thought and most often affects one side ([Craig, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35619928/)).

The maxillary sinus is the first sinus to become involved in infections of dental origin. These infections are often quite symptomatic and can spread to nearby sinuses and to tissues such as the skin and the eye socket ([Mularczyk and Welch, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39142996/)).

The numbers back up why this matters:

- A comprehensive review found that close to 30% of cases of one-sided maxillary sinusitis may have an underlying dental problem ([Psillas et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33384837/)).
- The same review noted that missing the dental cause often leads to sinusitis that will not clear up and can be linked to serious complications.

These were reviews of published research, not new trials. The Craig review also states that more well-designed studies are needed.

## What clues suggest the tooth is the source?

Doctors look for a group of findings, not one symptom. The Craig review lists features that raise suspicion of a dental source:

- Sinus infection on one side only, seen on a CT scan of the maxillary sinus
- Clear dental disease near the sinus on the same CT scan
- Pus seen on one side of the nose during a nasal endoscopy, which is a quick look inside the nose with a thin camera
- A foul smell
- Bacteria typical of the mouth found in a sinus culture

Outside the clinic, the clues you can notice are simpler. One-sided symptoms, a bad smell or taste, a history of dental work on an upper tooth, and sinus symptoms that keep returning all point toward a dental link. The same is true if a tooth problem was never fully dealt with.

Recent dental work matters too. Extractions, implants and root treatments on upper teeth sit near the sinus floor. The imaging review notes that the anatomy of this area matters for both complications of oral surgery and for deliberate sinus surgery ([Whyte and Boeddinghaus, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31386556/)).

## What if both sides ache and the whole face feels heavy?

That pattern fits sinus inflammation from a cold or allergies better than a tooth problem. Swollen nasal lining blocks the normal drainage of the sinuses, and pressure builds. Sinus infections that start in the nose usually begin with nasal inflammation, followed by blockage of the sinus drainage area ([Whyte and Boeddinghaus, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31386556/)).

If the ache fades as the congestion clears, the sinus was the likely source. If one tooth keeps hurting after the cold is gone, have it looked at.

## How is sinus tooth pain treated?

Treatment depends on the source. Sinus pain from a cold or allergy is managed as a sinus problem. Pain from a dental source needs the tooth handled too.

For odontogenic sinusitis, the research is fairly clear on a few points:

- Oral antibiotics are generally ineffective at resolving it, especially when the dental problem can be treated ([Craig, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35619928/)).
- When both the dental problem and the sinus infection are addressed, resolution can be expected in 90% to 100% of cases, per the same review.
- Fixing the tooth alone may be enough for some people. A significant share still need endoscopic sinus surgery. For people with heavy symptoms, doing sinus surgery first and then dental care is an option.
- Surgical treatment generally combines dental surgery with endoscopic sinus surgery to clear the infection and restore normal sinus drainage ([Psillas et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33384837/)).

Both reviews, and an expert consensus statement on multidisciplinary treatment of odontogenic sinusitis ([Lin et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38302479/)), stress teamwork between ENT doctors and dentists. That is why a dentist and a sinus specialist often work together on stubborn cases.

## What can help at home while the cause is sorted out?

Home care can ease symptoms but cannot tell you the cause. General steps that are well accepted:

- Rest and drink plenty of fluids.
- Use a warm compress on the cheek for comfort.
- Take over-the-counter pain relievers exactly as the label says, if they are safe for you.
- Sleep with the head raised a bit to help sinus pressure.
- If rinsing the nose, use distilled, sterile or previously boiled and cooled water, never straight tap water.

Do not use home care as a reason to delay a dental visit. A tooth infection does not go away on its own.

## When should you see a doctor or dentist?

See a dentist soon for a toothache that lasts more than a day or two, pain when biting, a swollen gum, or a cracked or broken tooth. See a doctor or ENT if sinus symptoms last more than about 10 days without improving, keep coming back, or stay on one side. A foul smell or taste, or a recent upper tooth extraction or implant followed by nasal drainage, also deserves a check.

Get urgent care right away for any of these:

- Swelling or redness around the eye, or changes in vision
- Swelling of the cheek or face that is spreading
- High fever with a severe headache
- Stiff neck or confusion
- Trouble breathing or swallowing

The reviews above note that dental-origin sinus infections can spread to the skin and eye area, which is why these signs matter.
