Antibiotics are one of the most common things people ask for when their face hurts and their nose is blocked. But most sinus infections start with a virus, and antibiotics do not work on viruses. This digest looks at what three major medical documents say about when antibiotics help, and what they cannot tell us.

## Do antibiotics help sinus infections?

Sometimes, but only for the minority caused by bacteria, and even then doctors often wait first. The sources below agree that the key step is telling a bacterial infection apart from a viral one.

Three documents shape current advice. Two are guidelines from otolaryngology groups. One is a review from the American College of Physicians (ACP) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). None is a single experiment. They are expert summaries of many studies, so they show where the evidence points rather than giving one clean number.

## What did the ACP and CDC advise in 2016?

The ACP and CDC advised reserving antibiotics for acute sinusitis that fits one of three patterns. Everyone else, including people with a common cold, should not get them.

**How it was done.** The authors (Harris, Hicks and Qaseem, for the ACP High Value Care Task Force and the CDC) wrote a narrative literature review. They searched the Cochrane Library, PubMed, MEDLINE and EMBASE through September 2015. They used professional society guidelines, then added meta-analyses, systematic reviews and randomized trials. The topic was acute respiratory tract infections in healthy adults, meaning people without chronic lung disease or weakened immune systems.

**What it found.** Its third piece of advice is about sinusitis. Antibiotics should be saved for people with one of these:

- Symptoms that last more than 10 days.
- Severe onset, with a high fever (above 39 °C, or about 102.2 °F) plus pus-like nasal discharge or facial pain for at least 3 days in a row.
- 'Double sickening.' This means a cold that lasted about 5 days and was getting better, then got worse again.

Its fourth piece of advice is plain: do not prescribe antibiotics for the common cold.

**Limits.** A narrative review is less strict than a systematic review. The authors chose which sources to include. It covers healthy adults only, so it does not settle questions for children, people with asthma or lung disease, or people with weak immune systems.

## What does the 2015 adult sinusitis guideline say?

The 2015 guideline strongly recommends that clinicians separate bacterial sinus infections from viral ones, and it widened the option of waiting before using antibiotics.

**How it was done.** The American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation updated its 2007 guideline (Rosenfeld and colleagues). The update used evidence from 42 new systematic reviews and, for the first time, added a consumer to the group.

**What it found.** Two strong recommendations stand out:

1. Clinicians should distinguish presumed acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (ABRS) from acute sinus inflammation caused by viral colds and non-infectious conditions.
2. Clinicians should confirm a chronic sinusitis diagnosis with objective evidence of inflammation. That can come from a look inside the nose, a nasal endoscope, or a CT scan.

The update also described 'expanded opportunities for watchful waiting (without antibiotic therapy) as initial therapy' of ABRS. Watchful waiting means a doctor agrees to hold off on antibiotics and check how you do over time.

**Limits.** The guideline text available here is cut off after those first recommendations. This digest therefore does not describe its other action statements, such as specific drug choices or timing.

## Is there a newer guideline?

Yes. In 2025 the same academy published an updated adult sinusitis guideline, and an executive summary appeared in *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery* (Payne and colleagues). It replaces the 2015 version.

**How it was done.** The group included specialists in ear, nose and throat surgery, infectious disease, family medicine, allergy and immunology, and advanced practice nursing, plus a consumer advocate. They followed the academy's 55-page guideline development manual (3rd edition). The update was needed because of new clinical trials and systematic reviews, and because the first guideline group had no consumer.

**What it found.** It contains 14 recommendations. They cover diagnostic accuracy, when to use tests such as nasal endoscopy, CT, and allergy or immune testing, and the 'judicious use of systemic and topical therapy.' It also tells clinicians to look for conditions that change management, including asthma, cystic fibrosis, a weakened immune system, and ciliary dyskinesia (a condition where the nose's tiny sweeping hairs do not work well).

**Limits.** The summary available here does not list the individual antibiotic recommendations. Readers should not assume the 2016 ACP/CDC thresholds were copied into it. Ask a clinician which guidance they follow.

## When might antibiotics make sense, and when is waiting reasonable?

According to the ACP and CDC review, waiting is reasonable early in an illness, and antibiotics are considered when the pattern suggests bacteria.

| Situation | What the 2016 ACP/CDC advice suggests |
|---|---|
| Common cold, a few days of runny nose and cough | No antibiotics |
| Symptoms improving after 5 to 7 days | No antibiotics |
| Symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement | Antibiotics can be considered |
| High fever (above 39 °C) with pus-like discharge or facial pain for 3+ days | Antibiotics can be considered |
| Cold improved for a few days, then got worse ('double sickening') | Antibiotics can be considered |

This table summarizes advice for healthy adults. It is not a diagnosis. A clinician decides based on your exam and history.

## What about chronic sinusitis and children?

The sources give far less direct guidance here, so caution is needed.

A 2013 review by Ocampo and Grammer on chronic rhinosinusitis (symptoms lasting more than 12 weeks) opens with one patient. She was a 50-year-old woman with asthma, nasal polyps and aspirin intolerance. Over 2 years she had many flare-ups that needed several antibiotic courses. She declined surgery. She then had aspirin desensitization and got partial relief from daily aspirin. This is one case, so it cannot show what works for most people. It does show that chronic sinusitis can involve more than infection, such as polyps and allergy-type inflammation.

A 2015 review of pediatric chronic sinusitis by Hamilos describes how children's disease can move from an acute bacterial infection to a persistent stage, and sometimes to a later stage dominated by eosinophils, a type of inflammatory cell. The author argues for earlier recognition and support from a sinus specialist. One stated reason is to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use and the antibiotic resistance it can promote. It is a narrative review, so it presents arguments, not trial results. Parents should work with a pediatrician on children's sinus problems, because the adult thresholds above were not written for kids.

## What can these documents not tell us?

They cannot tell any one person whether their infection is bacterial. They are not head-to-head trials of antibiotics versus no antibiotics, and the summaries here do not report effect sizes. They also lean on adults without other health conditions.

They do give a consistent message: match the treatment to the likely cause. Unneeded antibiotics can cause side effects such as stomach upset or allergic reactions, and they add to antibiotic resistance. That is general, well-established guidance, and it is why guidelines push for a careful diagnosis first.

For people at the Jersey Shore, this matters in busy summer weeks. A stuffy nose after a beach weekend or a cold picked up in a crowd is far more likely to be a virus than a bacterial infection. Giving it time, as the guidelines allow, is often reasonable.

## When to see a doctor

See a clinician if symptoms last more than 10 days without getting better, or if you have a high fever with thick discharge or facial pain for 3 days or more. Get help also if you get better and then clearly worse again.

Get urgent care for any of these:

- Swelling or redness around an eye
- Vision changes or double vision
- A severe headache or stiff neck
- Confusion
- Trouble breathing

See a doctor sooner if you have asthma, a weakened immune system, or symptoms that keep returning. Symptoms lasting more than 12 weeks may be chronic sinusitis, which needs its own work-up.
