Ragweed is the main cause of fall hay fever in New Jersey. It grows along roadsides, in vacant lots and at the edges of farms and suburban yards, and its light pollen travels on the wind. This guide covers when the season peaks, what helps day to day, and when to get medical help.

## When is ragweed season in New Jersey?

Ragweed season in New Jersey typically starts in mid-August, peaks in September, and tapers off in October. A hard frost usually ends it. Exact dates change with the weather each year, so treat these as a general pattern and not a forecast.

The season matters because it overlaps with back-to-school time. Kids and teens often notice symptoms in the first weeks of September and assume they have a cold. A "cold" that shows up every year at the same time, with itchy eyes and clear mucus, is more likely ragweed.

One study looked at Google searches for "ragweed" from 2011 to early 2017 across 15 countries with known ragweed seasons. The word "ragweed" was clearly identified in 12 of the 15. The authors suggested that search patterns may reflect real-life allergy seasons. The study did not measure pollen or symptoms directly, and the results varied a lot between countries. It is a hint about when people feel allergic, not a pollen count.

## What are the signs of a ragweed allergy?

The usual signs are sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, itchy eyes, and itching in the nose or throat that lasts for weeks in late summer and fall. Some people also get postnasal drip, a cough, or tiredness. Fever and thick yellow-green mucus are not typical of allergy.

Three clues point to ragweed over a cold:

- Symptoms start around mid-August and return each year.
- Eyes and nose itch, not just ache.
- Symptoms last more than 10 days with no fever.

People with asthma may find it harder to breathe during the season. That is a reason to check in with a doctor, covered below.

## How can you get through ragweed season at home?

The goal is to cut the amount of pollen that reaches your nose and eyes. Small habits add up over a six-to-eight-week season.

### Keep pollen out of the house

- Keep windows closed on dry, windy days, especially in the morning and around midday when pollen is often highest.
- Run the air conditioner on recirculate in the car and at home. Change the filter on schedule.
- Shower and wash your hair before bed so pollen does not end up on your pillow.
- Take shoes off at the door. Dry laundry in a dryer, not on an outdoor line.
- Wipe down pets that have been outside. Pollen rides in fur.

Shore towns can feel different from inland suburbs, but wind carries ragweed pollen long distances, so a beach house is not automatically a safe zone. Check local pollen reports and plan outdoor time with them in mind.

### Rinse and moisturize

Saline rinses can wash pollen out of the nose. If you use one, follow the [FDA's guidance on safe water for sinus rinsing](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/rinsing-your-sinuses-neti-pots-safely): use distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water, never plain tap water. For more background, see [what the studies show about nasal irrigation](https://healthynoses.com/nasal-irrigation-sinus-symptoms-studies/).

Allergy season can also leave the nose dry and sore. Antihistamines, decongestant sprays and heated indoor air as fall turns cold can all add to it. A nasal moisturizer such as Narisure can soothe and moisturize dry nasal passages, though it does not replace allergy medicine.

### Use medicines on a schedule

Many people do better starting allergy medicine before symptoms peak, for example in early August. A pharmacist can explain the over-the-counter options, including nasal steroid sprays, oral antihistamines and eye drops. Read labels, and ask before combining products, especially for children.

## Why does ragweed make some foods itch?

Some ragweed-allergic people get an itchy mouth or throat from certain raw fruits and vegetables. This is called oral allergy syndrome, or pollen-food allergy syndrome. The proteins in some foods look similar to pollen proteins, so the immune system reacts.

A 2018 review of oral allergy syndrome (OAS) found these key points:

- OAS was reported in about 5-8% of people with pollen allergy.
- About 1-2% of those with OAS had extreme reactions such as anaphylaxis.
- People with ragweed allergy may react to melon, cucumber, banana and zucchini.
- Symptoms usually start within moments of eating and fade a couple of minutes after swallowing.
- The raw food usually triggers symptoms, while the cooked version often does not.

The review summarized published literature, so it shows patterns, not what will happen to any one person. If a food ever causes swelling of the lips or tongue, trouble breathing, vomiting, or dizziness, treat it as an emergency and call 911.

## How is a ragweed allergy confirmed?

An allergist usually combines your history with a skin-prick test or a blood test. History matters: the timing of symptoms is a strong clue.

Be careful with blood tests alone. A 2022 study in Japan compared 132 adults with pollen symptoms against 127 without. It measured how well IgE blood levels predicted pollen allergy. The area under the curve was 0.87 for cedar, 0.82 for cypress, 0.63 for orchard grass and just 0.56 for ragweed. A score of 0.5 is chance, so ragweed's result was weak. The authors also found that cross-reactive carbohydrate components could cause false positives.

This study was in a Japanese population, where cedar is the major pollen, and it was a screening test, not a full workup. Still, it shows why a test result should be read by a specialist along with your symptoms.

## What long-term options exist for ragweed allergy?

People whose symptoms are hard to control can ask an allergist about immunotherapy, which gradually trains the immune system to tolerate an allergen. There are two main forms.

| Option | How it works | What a review found |
|---|---|---|
| Ragweed sublingual tablet | A tablet dissolves under the tongue | A review by Johns Hopkins–affiliated and German authors described pivotal trials showing statistically significant, clinically meaningful benefit for ragweed seasonal rhinitis-conjunctivitis, with or without milder asthma |
| Allergy shots (subcutaneous) | Injections of allergen extract at the doctor's office | A review of dosing studies found an effective dose is likely 5-20 µg of major allergen per injection, and maintenance shots are commonly given 1-2 times per month |

Both need a doctor's evaluation, and both require months of commitment. The tablet is taken at home after a first supervised dose, while shots are given in a medical setting. Reviews summarize the research; they do not predict your own result. Starting treatment usually happens well before the season, so fall is a good time to ask for a plan for next year.

## When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if symptoms last more than a few weeks, if over-the-counter medicine is not enough, or if allergy is disturbing sleep, school or work. Get care promptly for these:

- Wheezing, chest tightness or shortness of breath
- Facial pain or pressure with thick discolored mucus and fever, or symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improving
- Frequent nosebleeds or a nose that stays painfully dry
- Itchy mouth with any lip, tongue or throat swelling after eating (call 911 for breathing trouble)
