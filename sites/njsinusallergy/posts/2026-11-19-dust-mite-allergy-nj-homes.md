Dust mites are tiny creatures that live in household dust, and the allergy is a reaction to their droppings. It can cause a stuffy, runny nose all year long. In New Jersey, humid summers and sealed-up winter homes can both give mites a comfortable place to live.

## What is a dust mite allergy?

Dust mite allergy is an immune reaction to proteins in the droppings of house dust mites. The droppings are an indoor allergen, and breathing them in triggers symptoms, according to a StatPearls clinical review (PMID 32809553).

Dust mites are too small to see without a microscope. They feed on flakes of human skin. They live in places where those flakes pile up, such as mattresses, pillows, couches and carpet. Mites themselves do not bite. The problem is the allergen they leave behind.

The same review links house dust mites to allergic rhinoconjunctivitis (a stuffy, itchy nose with itchy eyes), allergic asthma and atopic eczema.

## Why are dust mites a problem in New Jersey homes?

Mites thrive in warm, humid air, and New Jersey has plenty of both in summer. Damp basements, shore houses that sit closed up between visits and older homes with wall-to-wall carpet can all hold more mites than a dry, bare-floored room.

Winter brings a different issue. Windows stay shut for months, so dust has nowhere to go. Many people notice a stuffy nose that gets worse indoors when the heat comes on.

Because mites live indoors, they do not follow the pollen calendar. If symptoms last through January as well as through ragweed season, dust mites are a reasonable suspect.

## What are the symptoms of dust mite allergy?

The common signs are sneezing, a runny or blocked nose, itchy or watery eyes and postnasal drip. Some people cough or wheeze, especially if they also have asthma.

Clues that point to mites rather than pollen:

- Symptoms happen year-round, not just in spring or fall.
- They are worse in the bedroom, first thing in the morning or after cleaning or making the bed.
- They get worse in a damp basement or a closed-up shore house.
- Symptoms improve when away from home for a few days.

Allergy testing, done by a doctor, is the only way to confirm that mites are the cause.

## Can dust mite allergy affect sleep?

It may. One small study found a link between a positive dust mite allergy test and breathing problems during REM sleep (PMID 32866850).

The details matter:

- **Design:** A retrospective look back at the records of 100 patients, 47 of whom tested positive for dust mites. Everyone had an overnight sleep study.
- **Result:** Compared with allergy-negative patients, the mite-positive patients were significantly more likely to have a moderate or severe breathing-disturbance score during REM sleep (15 or more events per hour). The odds ratio was 4.29, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.26 to 14.62.
- **Limits:** The patients were already being tested for sleep problems, so they are not a typical group. The study shows an association, not proof that mites cause the sleep problem. The wide confidence interval means the true size of the effect is uncertain.

It does fit what many people with allergies report: a blocked nose at night leads to poor rest.

## Where do dust mites live, and what actually helps?

Most of the allergen sits in the bedroom, so start there. The table below lists common hiding spots and practical steps. These are widely used, general recommendations.

| Where | Why mites like it | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Mattress and pillows | Warm, with plenty of skin flakes | Use zippered allergen-proof covers |
| Sheets and blankets | Hold skin flakes and moisture | Wash weekly in hot water (about 130°F / 54°C), then dry fully |
| Carpet and rugs | Deep fibers trap dust | Choose hard flooring in the bedroom if possible |
| Basement or shore house | Humid, closed-up air | Run a dehumidifier; aim for 30 to 50 percent humidity |
| Stuffed toys and upholstered furniture | Soft surfaces hold dust | Wash toys hot, limit them on the bed, wipe or vacuum furniture |
| Curtains and clutter | Collect dust | Use washable curtains and keep surfaces clear |

### How humid is too humid?

The usual advice is to keep indoor humidity between 30 and 50 percent. A cheap humidity meter (hygrometer) from a hardware store shows where a room stands. In summer, air conditioning and a dehumidifier help. In winter, avoid overdoing a humidifier, since extra moisture can favor mites.

### What about vacuuming and cleaning?

Vacuum weekly, ideally with a cleaner that has a sealed system and a HEPA filter, because ordinary vacuums can blow fine dust back into the air. Damp-dust hard surfaces rather than dry-dusting. If the allergic person is the one cleaning, a mask during chores can help.

## Does cutting down on mites really work?

Avoidance is still the first step in standard care, but its limits are real. The StatPearls review says the best strategy for allergic rhinitis is allergen avoidance first, combined with medicines and allergen immunotherapy (PMID 32809553).

There is a separate question: can mite reduction stop allergy from starting in the first place? A German guideline update reviewed 217 studies, including randomized trials, cohort studies and case-control studies (PMID 19878402). It dropped its earlier recommendation of reducing house dust mite exposure as a way to prevent allergies in at-risk children. That finding is about primary prevention in children who have not yet developed an allergy. It does not say that avoidance is pointless for someone who already has a mite allergy and symptoms.

A fair summary: cleaning and humidity control are low-risk and may ease symptoms, but they rarely remove mites completely. Most people need a mix of steps.

## What medicines and treatments help?

The StatPearls review lists the main options: antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and inhaled or nasal corticosteroids, along with allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or under-the-tongue tablets). It states that these treatments are effective and safe, but they have not been shown to change the course of mite-related allergic disease.

In plain terms, medicines can make symptoms easier to live with. They do not make the allergy go away.

Some of these medicines dry the nose. Heated winter air adds to the problem. A nasal moisturizer such as Narisure can soothe and moisturize dry nasal passages. For more on winter dryness, see [why your nose gets so dry in winter](https://healthynoses.com/why-your-nose-gets-dry-in-winter/).

## When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if:

- Nasal symptoms last more than a few weeks or keep coming back without a clear reason.
- Over-the-counter medicines and cleaning do not help enough.
- Sleep is poor, you snore loudly or you wake up gasping or unrefreshed.
- You wheeze, cough at night or feel short of breath. Get care urgently if breathing is hard.
- Facial pain, thick colored mucus or fever last more than 10 days, or get better and then worse.
- You want allergy testing to confirm mites or ask about immunotherapy.

If you or your child has trouble breathing, a swollen face or lips, or chest tightness, call 911 or go to the emergency room.
