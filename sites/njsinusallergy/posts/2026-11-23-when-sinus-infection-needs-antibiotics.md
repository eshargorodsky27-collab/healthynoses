Most sinus infections are caused by viruses, and antibiotics do nothing against viruses. Doctors keep antibiotics for a small group of cases that look bacterial. This guide explains which signs matter, what the research says, and when to get checked.

## When does a sinus infection need antibiotics?

Antibiotics are for sinus infections that are likely bacterial. A 2016 advice paper from the American College of Physicians and the CDC (Harris and colleagues, pmid 26785402) names three patterns for healthy adults:

1. **Symptoms last more than 10 days** without getting better.
2. **A severe start.** This means a high fever (above 39 °C, about 102 °F) plus pus-like nasal discharge or facial pain, lasting at least 3 days in a row.
3. **"Double sickening."** A cold lasts about 5 days and starts to improve, then symptoms get worse again.

The same paper says antibiotics should not be given for the common cold. It was a narrative review, not a new trial. It covered healthy adults, meaning people without chronic lung disease or weakened immune systems.

## Why don't most sinus infections need antibiotics?

Most cases start with a virus, and the body clears them without drugs. A 2020 review of acute rhinosinusitis (Jaume and colleagues, pmid 32495003) describes it as mainly an inflammatory disease triggered by a viral infection. Few cases end up with a bacterial infection. In most cases it is self-resolving, and treatment is aimed at symptoms.

The review also found that complications are uncommon and are not tied to whether antibiotics are used. It noted overuse of tests and prescriptions. It put the cost of one episode in Europe at over €1,000.

That is a review of existing evidence, and it draws on European practice. Still, it matches the U.S. guideline direction.

## How can you tell a viral sinus infection from a bacterial one?

It is hard. The 2020 review notes that bacterial and non-bacterial infections have similar symptoms. Doctors separate them by looking at a combination of signs and how the illness unfolds over time. Timing is the best clue.

| Pattern | More like a virus | More like bacteria |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Improving within about 10 days | Still no better after more than 10 days |
| Course | Gets steadily better after the first few days | Improves around day 5, then gets worse (double sickening) |
| Start | Mild to moderate symptoms | Fever above 39 °C with pus-like discharge or facial pain for at least 3 days in a row |
| Discharge color alone | Not a reliable clue by itself | Not a reliable clue by itself |

The cited guidance uses timing and severity, not mucus color. Thick or colored mucus can show up in a plain cold. Nobody should decide on antibiotics from tissue color.

## What is double sickening?

Double sickening is when a cold starts to get better, then gets worse again. The ACP and CDC advice describes it as worsening symptoms after a typical viral illness that lasted 5 days and was initially improving. This pattern is one of the three signs that can justify an antibiotic. A cold that simply drags on at the same level is judged by the 10-day mark.

## What does watchful waiting mean?

Watchful waiting means treating symptoms and checking back, without starting antibiotics right away. The 2015 adult sinusitis guideline (pmid 25832968) expanded the chance for this approach as first-line care for acute bacterial sinus infection. It also urged clinicians to tell presumed bacterial infection apart from viral colds and non-infectious causes, such as allergies.

That last point matters in New Jersey. Spring tree pollen and late-summer ragweed can cause stuffiness, pressure and drip that feel like an infection. Antibiotics do not help allergy symptoms.

A newer 2025 guideline update (pmid 40741969) replaces the 2015 version. Its summary lists 14 recommendations. They cover accurate diagnosis, when tests are needed, and careful use of both oral and nasal treatments.

## Which antibiotic do doctors use?

When a doctor decides a bacterial infection is likely, the choice depends on allergies and other health factors. One primary care review (DeCastro and colleagues, pmid 24439880) says patients diagnosed with bacterial sinusitis should be started on amoxicillin-clavulanate. If there is a penicillin allergy, it names doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone for non-pregnant patients.

This is a prescriber's decision. Leftover pills or a friend's prescription are not a safe substitute.

## What are the risks of taking antibiotics you do not need?

Antibiotics can cause side effects such as diarrhea, stomach upset and allergic reactions. Overuse also helps bacteria become resistant to the drugs. When a virus is the cause, the pills bring the risks without the benefit.

## What can you do while you wait?

Supportive care is the usual plan. General steps include:

- Drink plenty of fluids and rest.
- Use saltwater (saline) sprays or rinses with safe water, which means distilled, sterile or previously boiled and cooled.
- Use a warm shower or steam to ease stuffiness.
- Ask a pharmacist or doctor about pain relievers and other symptom medicines that suit your health and age.
- Limit decongestant sprays to a few days, since longer use can make congestion worse.

Note how long symptoms have lasted and how they are changing. That timeline is what a doctor will ask about. For more on how long an infection typically runs, see [how long a sinus infection lasts and when to worry](https://coastalsinus.com/how-long-sinus-infection-lasts-when-to-worry/).

## What if antibiotics do not work or symptoms keep coming back?

A failed course is a sign to look for another cause. The primary care review says patients who do not respond to antibiotics should be suspected of having chronic sinusitis, which may need more treatment, including endoscopic surgery, and referral to an ear, nose and throat (ENT) specialist. Chronic sinusitis is generally defined as symptoms lasting 12 weeks or more. The 2015 guideline says the diagnosis should be confirmed with objective proof of inflammation, using a nasal exam, nasal endoscopy or CT scan.

## Do some people need a different approach?

Yes. The ACP and CDC advice applies to healthy adults. The 2025 guideline summary puts special focus on conditions that change how sinus problems are managed. These are asthma, cystic fibrosis, a weakened immune system, and ciliary dyskinesia. People in these groups should not rely on general rules and should talk to a clinician early.

## When to see a doctor

Book a visit if symptoms last more than 10 days, if they improve and then get worse, or if you have a high fever with facial pain or pus-like discharge for 3 days in a row. Also go if you have asthma, a weakened immune system, or repeated infections.

Get urgent or emergency care for any of these:

- Swelling or redness around an eye, or vision changes
- Severe headache
- Confusion or unusual drowsiness
- Stiff neck
- Fever that stays very high

The primary care review warns that severe systemic symptoms, such as altered mental status or severe headache, should raise concern for a fungal sinus infection. It says this is rare and has a high death rate if not treated emergently. These signs call for fast action, not waiting.
