Mold allergy is a common reason noses run, itch and clog after a wet week. In New Jersey, humid summers, heavy storms, damp basements and flooding make mold exposure a real concern, both outdoors and inside the house. This guide covers why rain triggers symptoms, how to tell mold from other causes, and what to do about it.

## Why does mold allergy get worse after rain?

Rain and humidity help mold grow and release spores. Spores are tiny particles that float in the air, and an allergic person's immune system can react to them.

Outdoors, mold lives in leaf litter, soil, grass and rotting wood. Damp, warm weather lets it thrive. Indoors, the same thing happens wherever water lingers: basements, bathrooms, window sills, and around leaky pipes or roofs.

A 2016 review in the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice* (Baxi and colleagues) noted that high outdoor spore concentrations can cause asthma attacks in association with thunderstorms. The same review said most people appear to be relatively unaffected unless they are sensitized to specific types of mold. So a storm does not make everyone sick. It mainly affects people who are already allergic or who have asthma.

## What are the symptoms of mold allergy?

Mold allergy usually looks like other nasal allergies: sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, itchy eyes and nose, postnasal drip and a cough. Some people also get wheezing or chest tightness.

Here is how mold compares with other common triggers.

| Clue | Mold allergy | Pollen allergy | Common cold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timing | After rain, in damp rooms, or when disturbing old materials | Tied to tree, grass or ragweed seasons | A few days, then fades |
| Itchy eyes or nose | Common | Very common | Less common |
| Fever or body aches | No | No | Sometimes |
| Lasts | As long as exposure continues | Weeks during a season | About 7 to 10 days |
| Mucus color | Usually clear | Usually clear | Can turn yellow or green |

If symptoms get worse in one room, such as the basement, and ease when you leave, a damp indoor source is a good suspect. Similar symptoms can also come from dust mites, so see the [allergy and sinus library](/) for those guides.

## Does a damp home really affect health?

Yes, the link is consistent across many studies, though proof of direct cause is stronger for some outcomes than others.

A 2011 review in *Environmental Health Perspectives* (Mendell and colleagues) pulled together epidemiologic studies and meta-analyses up to late 2009. It found that visible indoor dampness or mold was consistently associated with asthma development and flare-ups, wheeze, cough, respiratory infections, allergic rhinitis and upper respiratory symptoms. The links appeared in both allergic and non-allergic people. The authors said the evidence strongly suggested that dampness or mold causes asthma flare-ups in children. The limit: most of the included studies observed groups of people rather than running experiments, so they show association more than proof for each outcome.

Other studies point the same way:

- **Children in South Africa (2019).** A cross-sectional study of 590 schoolchildren aged 9 to 11 in four informal settlements found that visible mold growth was linked with rhinitis (adjusted odds ratio 3.37). Dampness and mold were tied to a two- to threefold higher risk of upper and lower airway problems. A snapshot study like this cannot show which came first, and housing there differs from typical New Jersey homes.
- **Adults in Northern Europe (published 2026).** The RHINE cohort followed 8,407 adults who took part in both the 20-year and 30-year follow-ups. Home dampness or mold was linked to new allergic rhinitis (odds ratio 1.27). Combined home and work exposure was linked to higher odds of rhinitis (2.24) and doctor-diagnosed asthma (1.90). Exposure was self-reported on questionnaires, which can be imperfect.

The practical message is simple. Damp buildings and nose or chest symptoms go together, so drying and repairing matter.

## How fast does mold grow after a leak or flood?

Mold can begin to grow within 24 to 48 hours on wet surfaces. That is why speed matters more than any product.

The EPA and CDC give consistent advice on this:

1. **Stop the water source first.** Fix the leak, pump out standing water, or clear the gutter or drain.
2. **Dry everything within 24 to 48 hours.** Use fans, open windows when outdoor air is drier, and run a dehumidifier.
3. **Keep indoor humidity around 30 to 50 percent.** A cheap hygrometer shows the number.
4. **Clean hard surfaces** such as tile, metal and sealed wood with detergent and water, then dry them completely.
5. **Throw out soaked porous items** that cannot be dried quickly, such as carpet, ceiling tiles, insulation and stuffed items.
6. **Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.** The fumes are dangerous and can irritate airways.

Flood water can also carry sewage and other contaminants. That calls for more careful cleanup than rain leaking through a roof.

### What should I wear during cleanup?

Wear an N95 respirator, gloves and goggles. Disturbing moldy material sends spores into the air, which can set off symptoms in sensitive people. People with asthma or known mold allergy should ask someone else to do the work if possible. For large areas, such as more than about 10 square feet, the EPA suggests considering a professional.

## Should I test my home for mold?

Usually not. If you can see or smell mold, or know water got in, treat it as a moisture problem and fix it.

The 2011 review found only limited, mixed evidence for specific measured microbes in dust. Some were linked to worse health and some to better health. It concluded that preventing and fixing dampness is likely to reduce health risks, but current evidence does not support measuring specific indoor microbes to guide protective action. In plain terms: money is better spent on drying, repair and ventilation than on a spore count.

## What helps with symptoms during a wet, moldy stretch?

Avoiding exposure comes first. Then medicines can help.

- **Check forecasts and keep windows closed** on damp, stormy days if mold sets you off. Use air conditioning with clean filters.
- **Avoid raking wet leaves, mowing damp grass and working in compost piles.** These stir up spores.
- **Run a bathroom fan** during and after showers, and fix drips under sinks.
- **Ask about allergy medicines.** Antihistamines and nasal steroid sprays are commonly used for allergic rhinitis. A pharmacist or doctor can help you pick one.
- **Care for a dry nose.** Some allergy pills and sprays, plus heated or air-conditioned indoor air, can leave nasal passages dry. A nasal moisturizer such as Narisure soothes and moisturizes dry nasal passages and can be used alongside your usual routine.

For homes near the water, humidity can stay high even without storms. The Coastal Sinus guide to [summer humidity, mold and sinus flare-ups at the shore](https://coastalsinus.com/summer-humidity-mold-sinus-flare-ups-shore/) covers that setting.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if symptoms last more than a couple of weeks, keep returning after storms, or disrupt sleep or school and work. An allergist can use skin or blood tests to find out whether mold is actually the trigger.

Get medical care quickly for:

- Wheezing, chest tightness or shortness of breath, especially in a child or someone with asthma
- Facial pain or pressure with thick discolored mucus, fever or symptoms lasting more than 10 days
- Swelling around the eyes, a severe headache or vision changes
- Symptoms that began after cleaning up a flood and are getting worse

Call emergency services for severe trouble breathing.

The 2016 review also lists allergic fungal sinusitis among the illnesses linked to fungus exposure, which is rare but a reason not to ignore sinus symptoms that will not clear.
