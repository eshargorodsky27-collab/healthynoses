Rinsing the nose with salt water is a common tip for hay fever and year-round nasal allergies. Two large evidence reviews, one from 2018 and one from 2025, looked at whether it works. The answer is cautiously positive, with some big limits.

## Does nasal saline irrigation help allergic rhinitis?

It may help. Both reviews found lower symptom scores in people who rinsed, but the trials were small, short and often differed from one another. That makes the results promising, not final.

Allergic rhinitis is the medical name for hay fever and similar nasal allergies. It causes a blocked nose, runny nose, sneezing and itching. Saline irrigation means flushing the nose with salty water. It can be done with a squeeze bottle, a pump, a spray, a nebuliser or a gravity-fed pot. Saline can be isotonic (about the same saltiness as body fluids, 0.9%) or hypertonic (saltier, such as 3%).

## What did the 2018 Cochrane review find?

The Cochrane review (Head and colleagues, 2018) found that saline irrigation may improve patient-reported symptom severity compared with no saline. The authors rated that evidence as low quality, so the true effect could differ.

### How the review was done

Cochrane reviews pool results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), where people are randomly assigned to different treatments. The team searched medical databases and trial registers up to 23 November 2017. They included trials of saline irrigation by any method, volume or saltiness. They compared it with no saline or with other drugs. They also included trials where everyone took allergy medicine, such as a nasal steroid spray or an oral antihistamine, and only some added saline.

The review included 14 studies with 747 participants:

- Seven studies were in children (499 children).
- Seven studies were in adults (248 adults).
- No study followed people for longer than three months.

### What it found

Seven studies (112 adults and 332 children) compared saline with no saline. They used different symptom scoring systems. To combine them, the authors used a standardised mean difference (SMD), a way to put different scales on one common scale. A negative number means milder symptoms.

- **Up to four weeks:** SMD -1.32 (95% confidence interval -1.84 to -0.81; 407 participants; 6 studies; low quality).
- **Four weeks to three months:** SMD -1.44 (95% confidence interval -2.39 to -0.48).

Both intervals sit below zero, which points toward benefit. The wide intervals show a lot of uncertainty, especially at the later time point. The abstract text available here is cut off before the results for side effects such as nosebleeds and for comparisons with other drugs. So those findings are not covered in this digest.

## What did the 2025 meta-analysis find about hypertonic saline?

The 2025 meta-analysis (Singh and colleagues) found that 3% hypertonic saline lowered nasal symptom scores in both adults and children compared with controls. It did not clearly beat plain isotonic saline for reducing antihistamine use.

### How the review was done

This team asked a narrower question: is the saltier 3% solution better? They searched PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane Central through 8 May 2024. They included only RCTs that compared 3% hypertonic saline with a control, either 0.9% saline or no saline. Nine RCTs with 645 patients qualified. Follow-up ranged from 4 weeks to 2 months. The average age was 35.49 years in the adult trials and 9.3 years in the child trials.

### What it found

- **Adults:** symptom scores were lower with hypertonic saline (mean difference -2.09; 95% CI -3.86 to -0.33).
- **Children:** symptom scores were also lower (mean difference -0.97; 95% CI -1.51 to -0.44).
- **Antihistamine use:** lower with hypertonic saline than with control overall (odds ratio 0.39; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.70).
- **Versus isotonic saline alone:** no significant difference in antihistamine use (odds ratio 0.69; 95% CI 0.41 to 1.16).

That last line matters. When the control group rinsed with plain 0.9% saline, hypertonic saline did not clearly do better on medicine use. So the extra salt is not proven to be the active ingredient. Rinsing itself may do much of the work.

## How do the two reviews compare?

| Feature | Cochrane review (2018) | Meta-analysis (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Trials included | 14 RCTs | 9 RCTs |
| People | 747 (499 children, 248 adults) | 645 |
| Question | Does saline irrigation help versus no saline or other treatments? | Does 3% hypertonic saline beat controls? |
| Follow-up | Up to 3 months | 4 weeks to 2 months |
| Main result | Lower symptom severity versus no saline | Lower symptom scores in adults and children |
| Key caution | Low-quality evidence | Adult results varied hugely (I2 = 97%) |

## What are the limits of this research?

The limits are real, and they affect how much weight to give the results.

- **Low-quality evidence.** The Cochrane authors rated key findings as low quality. That means future trials could change the picture.
- **Big differences between trials.** In the 2025 adult analysis, I2 was 97%. I2 measures how much results disagree beyond chance. A value that high means the adult trials gave very different answers, so the single pooled number should be read loosely.
- **Short follow-up.** The longest follow-up was three months. Nothing here shows what happens over years of daily rinsing, or across a whole season.
- **Mixed methods.** Trials used different devices, volumes and saltiness levels. The reviews cannot say which exact routine is best.
- **Self-reported symptoms.** Most outcomes are scores that people give themselves. That is useful, but it is open to bias when people know they are getting a rinse.
- **Rinse versus medicine.** The reviews do not show that rinsing replaces allergy medicine. Some trials added saline on top of drugs.

## What does this mean for people with allergies in New Jersey?

Saline rinsing looks like a low-risk add-on that may take the edge off symptoms. New Jersey has distinct allergy seasons: tree pollen in spring, grass pollen in late spring and early summer, and ragweed in late summer and fall. Damp shore homes can also add indoor mold and dust mite triggers. A rinse after time outdoors in pollen season is a reasonable thing to try. Flushing the nose may physically wash out pollen and mucus, though these reviews did not test that idea directly.

A few practical points follow from the research and from standard safety advice:

- Treat saline as a helper, not a replacement for medicine a doctor has recommended.
- Use distilled, sterile or previously boiled and cooled water. Never use plain tap water. The FDA explains the risks of rinsing with unsafe water.
- Clean and dry the device after each use.
- Ask a doctor before using a hypertonic mix, especially for children. The 2025 trials used 3% solutions, and the studies do not show that stronger is safer or better.

## When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if allergy symptoms last more than a few weeks despite treatment, or if they disrupt sleep, school or work. Get checked sooner for any of these:

- Thick yellow or green discharge with facial pain and fever.
- Symptoms that last longer than 10 days without improving, or that get worse after a brief improvement.
- Repeated nosebleeds, or a rinse that causes pain or bleeding.
- Wheezing, chest tightness or trouble breathing.
- A baby or young child with nasal symptoms. Do not start rinses without medical advice.

One-sided blockage, loss of smell or symptoms that never change with the seasons can point to a cause other than allergies, so they deserve an exam.
