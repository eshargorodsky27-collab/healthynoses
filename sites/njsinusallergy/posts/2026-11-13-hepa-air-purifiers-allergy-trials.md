People with allergies often ask whether a HEPA air purifier is worth buying. Two randomized trials and one large review give a partial answer. The short version: purifiers clearly clean the air, but the proof that this makes allergy symptoms better is mixed.

## Do HEPA air purifiers help allergy symptoms?

Sometimes, and mostly in small, short studies. In one trial, symptoms dropped. In another, symptoms people rated themselves did not change, but they used less allergy medicine. A purifier is best seen as one helper, not a stand-alone fix.

## What is a HEPA filter, and what does it catch?

A HEPA filter is a dense mesh that traps tiny airborne particles. True HEPA filters are rated to catch at least 99.97% of particles about 0.3 microns wide. Pollen, pet dander and dust mite debris are mostly in that size range or larger.

The filter only works on air that passes through it. Allergen that has settled into carpet, bedding or sofas stays put until it is stirred up.

## What did the cat allergy chamber trial find?

This trial tested whether a HEPA H13 air cleaner could reduce reactions to cat allergen in a controlled room. It was published in *Respiratory Medicine* in 2025 (PMID 40246248).

### How it was done

- **Who:** 30 adults with mild cat-triggered asthma (GINA steps 1-2), with or without allergic rhinoconjunctivitis (itchy, runny nose and eyes).
- **Design:** Single-center, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group. Neither the patients nor the staff knew who had the active air cleaner and who had a placebo unit.
- **Setting:** An environmental exposure chamber in Strasbourg, France. Each person sat for 2 hours in air containing 80 ng/m3 of cat allergen.
- **Steps:** At a first chamber visit, people had to show an early asthma response. At a second exposure, the active or placebo air cleaner was running.

### What it found

- Cat allergen in the air fell from 79.6 ng/m3 to 14.2 ng/m3 with the air cleaner.
- The early asthma response came later in the active group (p = 0.02). After 50 minutes, none of the active-group patients had one, compared with 53.3% in the placebo group.
- Lung function (FEV1) dropped in 40% of the active group's early responses versus 73.3% in the placebo group. That gap was not statistically significant (p = 0.06).
- Nose and eye symptoms fell by 52.2%. The mean total symptom score was 1.6 with the air cleaner and 3.3 with placebo (p = 0.03).

### Limits

Only 30 people took part, and the study used one brand of air cleaner. The exposure lasted 2 hours in a chamber, not weeks in a real home. The study was funded by Dyson, which makes the device tested.

## What did the home trial for dust mite allergy find?

This trial moved purifiers into real homes. It was a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in Korea, published in the *Yonsei Medical Journal* in 2020 (PMID 32734732).

### How it was done

Adults with allergic rhinitis caused by house dust mites were randomly given either working HEPA purifiers or mock-up units that looked real but did not clean the air. Two units ran in each home, one in the living room and one in the bedroom, for 6 weeks. The main goal was to see whether symptom scores and medication scores improved. Other goals included quality of life, a visual analog scale (a rating of how bad symptoms feel) and indoor particle levels.

### What it found

- Medication scores improved significantly with working purifiers compared with placebo.
- Symptom scores, visual analog scale scores and quality-of-life scores did **not** differ between groups.
- Fine particles (PM2.5) in the bedroom dropped by up to 51.8%. PM2.5 in the living room and PM10 in both rooms also fell significantly.

### Limits

The abstract does not report how many people took part, so the size of the study is unclear. Six weeks is short for an allergy that lasts all year. Particle counts are not the same as dust mite allergen counts, so a cleaner room by this measure does not prove less allergen exposure. Several authors worked for the air care division of an appliance maker, which makes purifiers.

## What does the Cochrane review say about HEPA filters and dust mites?

It says the evidence is weak overall. The review (PMID 20614426) looked at ways to cut dust mite exposure for people with perennial allergic rhinitis. The last search was December 2009.

It found nine randomized trials with 501 participants in total. Only two trials, both on mite-proof bedding covers, were good quality. The other seven were small and of poor quality. Two of the nine trials tested HEPA filters, and others tested acaricides (mite-killing products) or bedding covers.

Seven of the nine trials showed less dust mite load with the measures tested. But the review said the trials as a whole were small and of poor methodological quality. It found that mite-proof bedding covers alone are unlikely to give a clinical benefit. No serious side effects were reported. The abstract does not break out results for the HEPA trials alone, so this review cannot say how well purifiers work on their own.

## What can these studies not tell us?

- **Long-term results.** None followed people for a full allergy season or year.
- **Pollen.** None tested tree, grass or ragweed pollen, which drive many New Jersey allergy seasons.
- **Other brands and sizes.** Results from one machine may not carry over to a cheaper or undersized one.
- **Independence.** Neither purifier trial was run free of links to a purifier maker.
- **Everyday life.** Open windows, pets on the couch and humid summers all change how a purifier performs.

## How can a purifier fit into a New Jersey home?

The trials suggest purifiers are reasonable as an add-on, especially for cat or dust mite allergy. General advice from allergy specialists:

- Put the unit where the most time is spent, often the bedroom, and keep that door closed.
- Pick a size rated for the room. A small unit in a large living room will do little.
- Replace filters on the maker's schedule.
- Avoid devices that make ozone, which can irritate the airways.
- Pair it with other steps, such as washing bedding in hot water weekly and keeping pets out of the bedroom.
- Dust mites thrive in humid air, and NJ summers are humid. A dehumidifier or air conditioning that keeps indoor humidity under about 50% can help.

For general background on allergy triggers and symptoms, see the NIH's MedlinePlus pages on [hay fever](https://medlineplus.gov/hayfever.html) and [allergy](https://medlineplus.gov/allergy.html).

## When should you see a doctor about allergy symptoms?

A purifier does not replace medical care. See a doctor if:

- Symptoms last more than a few weeks or keep coming back.
- Over-the-counter medicines are not enough.
- You have wheezing, chest tightness or shortness of breath. Seek urgent care if breathing is hard.
- Facial pain, thick colored discharge or fever last more than 10 days or get worse.
- Allergies disrupt sleep, school or work.
