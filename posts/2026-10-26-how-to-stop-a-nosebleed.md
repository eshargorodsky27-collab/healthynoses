Nosebleeds look dramatic, but they are one of the most common things that happen to a nose. According to the 2020 national guideline written for doctors, at least 60 percent of people in the United States get one at some point, and only about 6 percent of them ever need medical help. The rest stop at home, as long as you use the right technique.

## Where do most nosebleeds come from?

**Most come from the front of the nose, on the wall between the nostrils.** This spot has a dense web of tiny blood vessels sitting just under a thin lining. When that lining dries out, cracks or gets scratched, a vessel breaks and bleeds.

Common triggers include:

- Dry indoor air from heating in winter
- Picking or rubbing the nose
- Blowing hard during a cold or allergy season
- Colds and sinus infections that irritate the lining
- Some medicines, including blood thinners and overused decongestant sprays

## How do you stop a nosebleed?

**Sit up, lean forward and pinch the soft part of your nose firmly for at least 10 minutes.** That's the core of it. Here is the full method:

<figure class="wide"><img src="/images/diagram-nosebleed-steps.svg" alt="Four steps to stop a nosebleed: sit up and lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose, hold for 10 minutes without checking, then rest and avoid blowing" loading="lazy"></figure>

1. **Sit up straight and lean slightly forward.** Keeping your head above your heart lowers pressure in the vessels, and leaning forward lets blood drain out instead of down your throat.
2. **Pinch the soft part of your nose.** Use your thumb and index finger to squeeze both nostrils closed just below the hard, bony bridge. Pressing on the bone does nothing; the bleeding spot is lower down.
3. **Hold firm pressure and don't peek.** Breathe through your mouth and keep squeezing for a full 10 minutes by the clock. The national guideline tells doctors to start with firm, sustained compression of the lower third of the nose, and letting go to check is the most common reason bleeding starts again.
4. **Rest afterward.** For the next day or so, avoid blowing your nose, heavy lifting, bending over and hot drinks, which can all dislodge the fresh clot.

A cold pack on the bridge of the nose can feel soothing, but it is no substitute for pinching.

## What should you not do?

| Myth | Why to skip it |
|---|---|
| Tilt your head back | Blood runs down the throat, which can cause coughing or vomiting and hides how much you are bleeding |
| Lie flat | Same problem as tilting back |
| Stuff tissue deep into the nostril | Pulling it out can tear the clot and restart bleeding |
| Check every minute | Each release lets the clot break |
| Blow hard right after | The clot is fragile for several hours |

## How can you prevent nosebleeds?

**Keep the lining of your nose moist.** Dry, cracked tissue bleeds easily, which is why nosebleeds cluster in winter. A study of 476 emergency visits in New Brunswick, Canada, found the most nosebleeds happened in winter, and visits rose as daily humidity fell.

- Run a humidifier in your bedroom when the heat is on
- Use saline spray or an oil-based nasal moisturizer to keep the lining supple
- Keep fingernails short, especially for kids who pick
- Blow gently, one nostril at a time

## When should you get medical help?

Call your doctor or get urgent care if:

- Bleeding keeps going after 20 minutes of steady pressure
- The bleeding is heavy, or you feel dizzy, faint or short of breath
- It started after a blow to the head or face
- You take blood thinners and can't get it to stop
- Nosebleeds happen often, come from the same side each time, or come with a blocked nose on one side

Frequent nosebleeds are worth a check even when each one is small. A doctor can look for a fragile vessel that can be treated, and can review your medicines.
