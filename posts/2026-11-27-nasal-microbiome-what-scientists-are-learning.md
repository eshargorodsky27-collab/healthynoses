Every nose is home to a crowd of microbes. Scientists call this crowd the nasal microbiome, and they are working out which members help, which harm, and when that changes. This digest looks at three recent review papers that touch on the topic, how each was done, and what they can and cannot tell readers.

## What is the nasal microbiome?

The nasal microbiome is the community of bacteria, viruses and fungi that live in the nose. Most of these microbes sit quietly on the nasal lining and do no harm.

The nose is the front door of the airway. Air, dust, pollen and germs all pass through it. It makes sense that microbes settle there, and that the immune system keeps a close watch on them.

Two ideas guide most of the research:

- **Colonization** means a microbe lives in the nose without causing symptoms.
- **Infection** means a microbe causes illness, such as a sinus infection.

The same species can do both. That is one reason the topic is hard to study.

## What does a 2025 review say about Staphylococcus aureus in the nose?

A 2025 review in *Antibiotics* says the nose is one of the main places where *Staphylococcus aureus* settles, and that these nasal bacteria can be a source of later infections. It is a review, so it summarizes earlier work rather than running a new experiment.

### How it was done

The authors searched three research databases: PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. They pulled together what is known about how *S. aureus* colonizes the body, causes disease, forms biofilms (sticky bacterial layers) and resists antibiotics. The paper does not report a single patient group or sample size, because it combines many studies.

### What it found

- Nasal, skin and oropharyngeal (throat) colonization are described as reservoirs for invasive infections.
- The bacteria use surface proteins to stick to tissue and secrete toxins. They also adapt how they get iron and nutrients.
- Biofilms and gene swapping between bacteria make antibiotic resistance worse, especially in methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* (MRSA).
- Decolonization strategies, meaning attempts to clear the bacteria from the body, have fallen short in part because they do not target more than one body site.
- The authors call for new anti-virulence therapies, vaccines and better diagnostics.

### The limits

The review is mostly about *S. aureus* as a pathogen. It says less about the many harmless microbes in the nose. It also cannot tell a reader whether carrying the bacteria means they will get sick. That risk differs from person to person.

## What does the Lancet RSV review say about the respiratory microbiome?

A 2024 Lancet review mentions the respiratory microbiome only briefly, as an open question. Its main subject is respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in young children. The paper still shows why scientists care about microbes in the airway.

### How it was done

The authors searched the medical literature from the past 15 years. They then picked the most important papers and recent reviews on each subtopic. They describe this second step as non-systematic, so it reflects expert judgment rather than a strict formula.

### What it found

- Each year, about 33 million RSV lower respiratory infections occur in children under 5. These lead to about 3.6 million hospitalizations and 118,200 deaths.
- There is no evidence-based treatment for RSV, only supportive care.
- RSV is now vaccine-preventable in young children. A long-acting antibody and a maternal vaccine were approved in 2023.
- The authors say future "vaccine probe studies" could answer open questions, including how preventing RSV affects the respiratory microbiome and long-term outcomes.

### The limits

The paper does not measure the nasal microbiome. It only names it as something future studies should look at. It also focuses on lower respiratory infection, which affects the lungs, not the nose alone.

## What does an eosinophil review add?

A 2021 state-of-the-art review in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* adds that the immune system and the microbiome influence each other. It covers eosinophils, a type of white blood cell. These cells fight some infections. They also play a harmful role in diseases including asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps.

### What it found

- Newer research shows eosinophils also help with normal body processes. The abstract lists epithelial (lining) and microbiome regulation among them.
- Treatment of eosinophil-driven disease has long relied on glucocorticoids. Newer biologic drugs that target signals such as IL-5 and IL-4/IL-13 are now approved.
- Some patients still do not get enough symptom control, so researchers want to understand other factors that keep disease active.

### The limits

This is an expert review, not a trial. It does not show that changing nasal microbes changes polyps or sinus disease. It only points to a link worth studying.

## How do the three reviews compare?

All three are reviews, which gather and interpret earlier studies. None reports new measurements of the nasal microbiome in a group of people.

| Review | Year | Main focus | What it says about the nose or microbiome |
|---|---|---|---|
| *S. aureus* pathogenesis (*Antibiotics*) | 2025 | One bacterium | The nose is a colonization reservoir. Single-site decolonization has fallen short. |
| Severe RSV in children (*Lancet*) | 2024 | One virus | The respiratory microbiome is a knowledge gap for future studies. |
| Eosinophils (*Mayo Clin Proc*) | 2021 | One immune cell | Eosinophils take part in epithelial and microbiome regulation. |

## What does this mean for readers?

The reviews support a few cautious points:

1. **A microbe in the nose is not always a problem.** Colonization is common and often silent.
2. **Killing everything is not the goal.** The nose is a community, and *S. aureus* is only one member.
3. **Antibiotics are not a casual fix.** Resistance is a real concern, as the 2025 review stresses. Antibiotics for sinus symptoms should come from a clinician. See [when a sinus infection needs antibiotics](https://njsinusallergy.com/when-sinus-infection-needs-antibiotics/) for the usual signs.
4. **Simple habits still make sense.** Wash hands, avoid picking at the nose, and do not open sores inside the nostril.

## What can't this research tell us?

The research cannot yet say how to build a "healthy" nasal microbiome. These reviews do not test any spray, oil, rinse or supplement for changing nasal microbes. They also do not give a target mix of microbes to aim for.

Big questions are still open. Does a change in nasal microbes cause sinus disease, or does the disease change the microbes? Does the answer differ for kids, older adults and people with allergies? Future studies that follow people over time are needed.

## When should you see a doctor?

See a doctor if nose or sinus symptoms are severe, long-lasting or keep coming back. Specific signs include:

- Congestion, facial pain or thick discharge lasting more than 10 days, or getting worse after a first improvement.
- Symptoms lasting 12 weeks or more.
- A painful, swollen or pus-filled boil or sore inside the nostril.
- A fever, swelling around the eyes, a severe headache or vision changes. These need urgent care.
- Repeated sinus infections, or bleeding that is frequent or hard to stop.

For a baby or young child, get medical care quickly for fast or labored breathing, a bluish color, or trouble feeding. These can be signs of a serious lung infection such as RSV.
