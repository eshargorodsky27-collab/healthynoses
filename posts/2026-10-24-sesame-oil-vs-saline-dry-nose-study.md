Most advice for a dry nose starts with saline spray. It is cheap, gentle and easy to find. But a small, well-designed trial from Sweden is one of the reasons doctors also recommend oil-based nasal moisturizers.

## The question

Researchers at the County Hospital in Skellefteå, in northern Sweden, wanted to know whether pure sesame oil worked any better than isotonic saline (salt water at the same concentration as body fluids) for people whose nasal lining was dry.

Skellefteå made a good test site. During the study, from mid-March to the end of May 2000, the outdoor absolute humidity was low, so participants were dealing with real seasonal dryness.

## How the study worked

The team enrolled 79 people with nasal mucosa dryness and used a crossover design:

1. Half the group used sesame oil for 14 days, then switched to saline for 14 days.
2. The other half did the same in reverse.

Because everyone tried both, each person acted as their own comparison. That cuts out a lot of the noise that comes from comparing different people.

Every evening, participants rated three symptoms on a visual scale: dryness, stuffiness and crusting.

## What they found

| Measure | Sesame oil | Saline |
|---|---|---|
| Dryness | Improved significantly more | Smaller improvement |
| Stuffiness | Improved significantly more | Smaller improvement |
| Crusting | Improved significantly more | Smaller improvement |
| People reporting their nose felt better | About 8 in 10 | About 3 in 10 |

All three differences were statistically strong. Adverse events were described as few and temporary.

The authors concluded that for nasal dryness caused by a dry winter climate, pure sesame oil was significantly more effective than saline.

## Why oil might make the difference

Saline wets the surface, but water evaporates, especially in dry air. An oil spreads into a thin film that slows evaporation from the lining underneath. That is the same reason lip balm works better than licking your lips.

## What this study can't tell you

- **It was small and local.** 79 people in one town over one spring. Results in other climates and age groups could differ.
- **Symptoms were self-rated.** Participants knew which product they were using, since oil and saline feel very different. That can nudge ratings.
- **It tested one ingredient.** The study used pure sesame oil. It says nothing directly about blends or specific brands.
- **It was about dryness.** It did not test nosebleeds, infections or chronic sinus disease.

## What it means in practice

If saline alone has not been enough for a dry, crusty nose in winter, this trial gives reasonable support for trying an oil-based nasal moisturizer made for use in the nose. It pairs well with a bedroom humidifier. Seasonal research also shows nosebleed visits peak in winter as humidity drops, so keeping the lining moist is worth the small effort.

Anyone with a sesame allergy should avoid sesame-based products, and persistent or one-sided symptoms deserve a doctor's look.
