A dry, stuffy or crusty nose is one of the most common complaints from people who use CPAP. CPAP is a machine that blows pressurized air to keep the airway open during sleep. The dryness is not in your head. Lab and clinical studies point to real changes in how the nose holds moisture, and most fixes are simple.

## Why does CPAP make my nose dry?

CPAP keeps blowing air past the nose lining all night, and the pressure may also reduce how much moisture the lining can supply. Both effects can leave the nose dry, irritated or congested.

The inside of the nose is coated with a thin layer of watery fluid and mucus. This layer warms and moistens the air you breathe. It also traps dust and germs. When a machine pushes a steady flow of air through, that layer has to work harder.

Two lab studies looked at the pressure itself:

- **Bovine trachea study (2014).** Researchers in New Zealand used whole windpipe tissue from cattle. They simulated breathing at different pressures. At normal air pressure, the tissue supplied about 31.2 microliters of surface liquid per square centimeter per hour. At 5 cm H2O above normal, a common CPAP pressure, supply dropped by 22%. The test used 8 samples. It was a pilot study on animal tissue, not on living human noses.
- **Human cell study (2020).** Researchers grew human nasal and bronchial lining cells in a dish and applied CPAP-like pressure. The pressure compressed the cells and made them less permeable. It also lowered their mucus secretion rate. That left the surface liquid drier and changed its mucus-to-water balance. Cells in a dish are not a whole nose, so the results are a clue, not proof of what happens in every person.

Together, these studies suggest dryness is not just from the air being dry. The pressure may also slow the nose's own moisture supply.

## How common are nose symptoms with CPAP?

Nose and upper-airway symptoms are very common, especially in the first weeks. Exact rates depend on the person and the setup.

Two studies give a sense of scale:

- **Healthy volunteers (2020).** Seventeen healthy men with no nasal symptoms used CPAP at 12.5 cm H2O through a nasal mask for 3 hours, without humidity. Twelve of the 17 (70.5%) had at least one nose symptom afterward. The study was small, included only men and was very short. Only 7 of them came back to try humidified CPAP.
- **People with sleep apnea (2016).** A Finnish team followed 536 adults with obstructive sleep apnea for one year after they started CPAP. Before treatment, average scores on a 0 to 100 scale were about 30 for nasal stuffiness and 16 for runny nose. Mouth dryness averaged about 44.

The cell study above also lists sneezing, runny nose, post-nasal drip, congestion and nosebleeds as side effects patients report with CPAP.

## Does a CPAP humidifier really help?

For most studies, yes. Adding humidity to the air tends to reduce nose dryness and congestion, though it does not fix everything for everyone.

Here is what the research found:

- In the 17-man study, the humidified session was not linked to early nasal inflammation, and symptoms were fewer. The researchers measured white blood cells in nasal wash fluid as a sign of inflammation. They said more research is needed.
- In the Finnish one-year study, people who used humidification did not see their runny-nose scores worsen (change of -0.4, which is basically no change). Their nasal stuffiness scores improved by about 5 points. Without humidification, runny-nose scores rose by 11.5 points and stuffiness rose by 8.5 points. This was an observational follow-up. The abstract does not say people were randomly assigned to humidity or not, so other differences between the groups could play a part.
- A German randomized crossover trial of 44 people compared standard CPAP with CPAP using a heated breathing tube humidifier. Nose and throat dryness was reduced right away and over the first weeks. Cold face feeling improved. Waking up from wetness on the face was slightly more common with the heated tube. Quality of life and how many hours people used CPAP did not change after 4 weeks.

Not every study agrees. The human cell study found the cells were just as inflamed with or without added humidity. That is a dish experiment, and the clinical studies measured how people actually felt. Still, it is a reminder that humidity may ease symptoms without undoing every effect of pressure.

## What can I try to keep my nose comfortable on CPAP?

Start with humidity and mask fit, then add small comfort steps. Change one thing at a time so you can tell what works.

| Step | What it does | Things to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Turn on the heated humidifier | Adds moisture to the air before it reaches the nose | Raise the setting a little at a time |
| Use a heated tube, if your machine has one | Keeps air warm and moist all the way to the mask | May cause some wetness on the face |
| Fix mask leaks | Leaks blow extra air across the nose and can dry it | Ask the supplier to refit the mask |
| Use distilled water and clean the chamber | Keeps the humidifier working well | Follow the maker's cleaning directions |
| Add room humidity | Eases dry air in winter or with air conditioning | Aim for about 30 to 50% humidity |
| Use a saline spray or gel before bed | Adds water-based moisture to the nostrils | Use sterile products made for the nose |

### Set up the humidifier

Most machines have humidity levels from low to high. If your nose feels dry, step up one level and give it a few nights. If you see water droplets in the tube or mask, called rainout, step it back down or use a heated tube. Use distilled water to limit mineral buildup.

### Check the mask fit

A mask that leaks can make the nose dry and sore. A mask that is too tight can also cause pressure sores near the bridge of the nose. Many suppliers will refit a mask for free. Ask about different sizes or a different style, such as nasal pillows or a full-face mask.

### Watch the room air

Winter heating makes bedroom air very dry. A room humidifier can add moisture. Keep it clean to avoid mold. Do not point the CPAP hose near a cold window or vent, which can add to rainout.

### Moisturize the nostrils

A small amount of moisture at the front of the nose can add comfort. Water-based saline sprays and gels are the usual first pick. An oil-based option such as Narisure is a nasal moisturizer that soothes and moisturizes dry nasal passages. If you use any oil or gel, put on a thin layer just inside the nostril and keep it off the mask cushion, since oils can affect the seal and wear down some mask materials. Check with the mask maker or your sleep clinic if unsure.

## Should I stop CPAP if my nose is dry?

No. Talk to your sleep clinic first, because dryness can usually be adjusted without giving up treatment.

The Finnish study found that symptoms before starting CPAP did not predict who would still be using it after a year. People who quit had smaller or no improvement in symptom scores during the start-up period compared with those who kept going. That is an association, not proof of cause. Still, it suggests that early comfort matters, and that fixing nose symptoms soon may help people stay on track.

Do not change the pressure on your own. Your doctor or sleep clinic sets it for a reason.

## When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or your sleep clinic if comfort steps do not work after one to two weeks, or sooner if any of these happen:

- Nosebleeds happen often or are hard to stop.
- Your nose is blocked on one side all the time.
- You see crusts, sores or cracks that do not heal.
- You have a lot of thick or colored mucus, facial pain or pressure, or a fever.
- Your nose symptoms are making you skip CPAP on many nights.

Sometimes allergies, a deviated septum or a sinus problem sit behind the stuffiness. A doctor can check and suggest the right next step, which might include changing your mask, your settings or your nose care routine.
