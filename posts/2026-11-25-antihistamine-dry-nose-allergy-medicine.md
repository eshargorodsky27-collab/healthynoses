Many people start an allergy medicine to stop sneezing and a runny nose, then notice a new problem: a dry, tight, sometimes crusty nose. That is a known side effect, not a sign that something is wrong with the pill. This guide explains why it happens, which medicines are more likely to cause it, and what helps.

## Do antihistamines dry out your nose?

Yes, they can. Antihistamines block histamine, the chemical that drives sneezing, itching and a runny nose. Many of them also reduce mucus overall, so the nose can end up drier than normal.

The drying is not the same for everyone. Some people notice nothing. Others feel it within a day or two of starting a medicine.

## Why does allergy medicine make the nose dry?

The nose lines itself with a thin layer of mucus. This layer traps dust and pollen, keeps tissue moist and helps tiny hairs (cilia) move debris out. When a medicine cuts mucus production, that layer gets thinner.

Older antihistamines also block a second signal in the body, called the cholinergic signal. This signal tells glands to make mucus and saliva. Blocking it is why these pills often cause dry mouth and dry eyes too. This effect is well known and is the main reason older antihistamines feel drying.

A thinner mucus layer can leave the nose feeling:

- Tight or scratchy
- Crusty inside
- More likely to bleed after a sneeze or a nose rub

## Which allergy medicines are most likely to cause a dry nose?

Not all allergy medicines dry the nose to the same degree. The table below gives a general picture. Individual reactions vary.

| Type of medicine | Common examples | Dryness risk |
|---|---|---|
| Older (first-generation) antihistamine pills | Diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine, doxylamine | Higher. Often also cause sleepiness and dry mouth. |
| Newer (second-generation) antihistamine pills | Cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine | Usually lower, but some people still feel dry. |
| Oral decongestants, often in "-D" products | Pseudoephedrine | Can dry the nose and mouth. |
| Nasal antihistamine sprays | Azelastine, olopatadine | Can cause local dryness or irritation in some people. |
| Nasal steroid sprays | Fluticasone, mometasone | Dryness, crusting or minor spotting of blood in some users. |

Many cold and "nighttime" products contain an older antihistamine, so it is worth reading the active ingredients on the label.

## What do studies say about antihistamines and nasal dryness?

The research here is limited, and the two studies available answer narrow questions. Neither one is a large trial built to measure dryness.

### A 2023 comparison of allergic rhinitis medicines

A retrospective study in the *World Journal of Clinical Cases* looked at 80 patients treated for allergic rhinitis at one hospital in Wuhan, China, between May 2019 and May 2021. One group received a leukotriene receptor antagonist. The other group received a glucocorticoid plus an antihistamine.

The researchers tracked symptoms and blood markers of inflammation. They also recorded side effects: nasal dryness, throat discomfort, a bitter taste and minor erosion of the nasal lining. These were reported together as one group of adverse reactions.

- The glucocorticoid plus antihistamine group had adverse reactions in 7.5% of patients.
- The leukotriene receptor antagonist group had adverse reactions in 27.5%.

This result does not show that antihistamines cause dryness. The group that took the antihistamine had fewer side effects overall. The study was retrospective, so the groups were not randomly assigned. It was small, came from a single hospital, and did not report nasal dryness on its own. What it does show is that nasal dryness is a recognized side effect in people treated for allergic rhinitis, and that rates differ by medicine.

### A 2018 trial of a nasal doxylamine spray

Doxylamine is an older antihistamine sold as an over-the-counter sleep aid. A phase I study in *Drugs in R&D* tested a nasal spray version in 24 adults with chronic, on-and-off sleep problems. Each person received a single dose of the spray (3.2, 6.3 or 12.7 mg) or a 25 mg tablet.

The spray did not get more of the drug into the body than the tablet. At the highest spray dose, blood levels were about 25% of those from the tablet. The most common side effects with the spray were nasal congestion, nasal dryness and frontal headache.

There are limits. The trial was small, used single doses and enrolled people with sleep problems, not allergies. Two of the authors worked for companies involved in the research. The finding is still a useful clue: an older antihistamine placed directly in the nose can leave it dry and stuffy.

## How can you ease a dry nose while taking allergy medicine?

These steps are general, low-risk and widely recommended.

1. **Drink enough fluids.** Dehydration makes dryness worse.
2. **Use saline spray or gel.** It adds moisture to the lining several times a day. A saline rinse can also wash out pollen and crust, but use only distilled, sterile or boiled-and-cooled water.
3. **Add humidity at home.** Aim for indoor humidity of about 30% to 50%. A small humidifier in the bedroom helps overnight. Clean it as directed, since dirty tanks can grow mold.
4. **Try an oil-based moisturizer.** Oils can slow moisture loss from the nasal lining. A nasal moisturizing oil such as Narisure soothes and moisturizes dry nasal passages. Use only a small amount, as the label says.
5. **Leave the crusts alone.** Picking or rubbing can start a nosebleed. Soften crusts with saline or a moisturizer first.
6. **Aim steroid sprays correctly.** Point the nozzle slightly outward, toward the side of the nose and away from the center wall (septum). This lowers the chance of irritation and spotting.

## Should you switch or stop the medicine?

Not on your own, especially if a clinician prescribed it. A pharmacist or doctor can often suggest a lower-drying option. For example, a newer antihistamine may replace an older one. Sometimes a different type of medicine is a better fit. Allergy symptoms can return quickly if a needed medicine is stopped without a plan.

Timing matters too. If a medicine is making the nose dry, it helps to note when the dryness began and which product was started just before.

## When should you see a doctor?

A mildly dry nose is common and usually manageable at home. A check-up is a good idea if any of these happen:

- Dryness lasts more than about 2 weeks despite saline, humidity and a moisturizer.
- Nosebleeds keep coming back, or one lasts longer than 10 to 15 minutes with steady pressure.
- Crusting is thick, painful or bad-smelling.
- There are sores, or blood or crusts on one side only.
- The sense of smell drops.
- Symptoms came on after starting a new medicine and keep getting worse.

Seek urgent care for a nosebleed that will not stop, heavy bleeding after a head injury, or trouble breathing.
