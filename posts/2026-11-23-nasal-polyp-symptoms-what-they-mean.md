Nasal polyps are soft, painless, noncancerous growths that form in the lining of the nose and sinuses. They often cause a stuffy nose and a weak sense of smell that will not go away. This guide explains what the symptoms mean, how they differ from a cold or allergies, and what recent research says about treatment.

## What are nasal polyps?

**Nasal polyps are swollen, teardrop-shaped bits of inflamed tissue that grow inside the nose or sinuses. They are not cancer, and they are not caused by dirt or poor hygiene.**

They look a bit like peeled grapes. Small ones may cause no symptoms. Larger ones, or clusters, can block airflow and trap mucus. Doctors usually call the long-term condition behind them chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, or CRSwNP.

A 2025 review in the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology* notes that CRSwNP is frequently marked by a "type 2" immune signature. This is a pattern of inflammation also seen in allergic conditions. Many people with polyps also have asthma.

## What are the most common nasal polyp symptoms?

**The most common symptoms are a blocked nose, a reduced or lost sense of smell, a runny nose or drip down the back of the throat, and pressure in the face.** They tend to build slowly and stick around.

- **Nasal blockage:** air does not move well through one or both sides, and breathing through the mouth becomes normal.
- **Loss or weakening of smell:** food tastes flat because much of "taste" is actually smell.
- **Runny nose or post-nasal drip:** mucus drains forward or down the throat.
- **Facial pressure or a dull ache:** around the forehead, cheeks or between the eyes.
- **Snoring or noisy breathing at night:** polyps narrow the airway.

Pain is usually mild. Sharp pain, fever or one-sided symptoms point to other problems (see the doctor section below).

## How are polyp symptoms different from a cold or allergies?

**A cold usually clears in 7 to 10 days, and allergies come with itching and sneezing. Polyp symptoms last 12 weeks or longer, with blockage and smell loss as the main problems.**

| Feature | Cold | Allergies | Nasal polyps |
|---|---|---|---|
| How long it lasts | About 7-10 days | While exposed to the trigger | 12+ weeks, often months or years |
| Main complaint | Runny nose, sore throat | Sneezing, itchy eyes and nose | Constant blockage, weak smell |
| Itching and sneezing | Sometimes | Very common | Less typical |
| Smell loss | Short-term | Mild, comes and goes | Common and persistent |
| Fever | Sometimes | No | Not typical |

These conditions can overlap. Someone with allergies can also have polyps. Only an exam can sort it out.

## Why do nasal polyps cause loss of smell?

**Smell nerves sit high up in the nose. Polyps and swelling can block air from reaching them, and the inflammation itself can dull them.**

This is why smell is a key outcome in polyp research. The 2025 meta-analysis of real-world biologic studies tracked smell identification test scores and loss-of-smell scores alongside congestion and polyp size. Smell loss is not always just a nuisance. It can mean missing smoke or spoiled food, so it is worth taking seriously.

## What causes nasal polyps?

**Polyps come from long-term inflammation of the nose and sinus lining. The exact trigger is often unclear.**

They are more common in people with asthma, and the 2025 review points to a shared type 2 inflammatory pattern. Some people have allergies or a sensitivity to aspirin-type medicines. Others have no obvious link at all. Polyps are not caused by a dry nose, though a very dry or irritated nose can add to crusting and discomfort on top of them.

## How do doctors confirm nasal polyps?

**A doctor looks inside the nose with a thin, lighted camera called an endoscope. A CT scan of the sinuses may be added to see how far the swelling reaches.**

Polyps are then scored. In the EVEREST trial, severe disease meant a nasal polyp score of 5 or more, with at least 2 in each nostril. The score is based on how much of the nasal airway the polyps fill. Doctors use it to track whether treatment is working.

## What treatments exist, and what does research say?

**Treatment usually starts with steroid nasal sprays and sometimes short courses of steroid tablets. Surgery or injected biologic drugs are options when symptoms stay uncontrolled.**

How a spray is used matters a lot. A practical guide is here: [how to use nasal steroid sprays the right way](https://njsinusallergy.com/nasal-steroid-spray-how-to-use/). Saline rinses are also commonly used alongside medicines.

For severe cases, newer research focuses on biologics. These are injected drugs that block specific parts of the type 2 immune response.

- **Review of approved options (2025):** the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology* review lists three biologics approved for CRSwNP: omalizumab, mepolizumab and dupilumab. It says biologics can improve quality of life and reduce the need for steroid tablets and sinus surgery. It also compares biologic therapy with surgery. The authors note one author has served on advisory boards for several drug companies.
- **Real-world meta-analysis (2025):** a review in *Allergy* pooled 64 real-world studies with 3,921 patients taking dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab or benralizumab. Polyp scores and symptom scores improved at most follow-up points, with dupilumab showing particularly notable effects. Few people stopped because of side effects. The results looked better than in earlier trials, but real-world studies have no placebo group and vary in quality. The authors call for long-term, high-quality prospective studies.
- **Head-to-head trial (EVEREST):** a phase 4 randomised, double-blind trial compared dupilumab with omalizumab in adults with severe uncontrolled CRSwNP and asthma, across 100 sites in 17 countries. It is the first direct comparison of this kind in respiratory biologics. The study was run with drug-company involvement.
- **Newer drugs in trials:** the ANCHOR-1 and ANCHOR-2 phase 3 trials tested depemokimab, a biologic given twice a year, in adults with inadequately controlled CRSwNP at 190 centres in 16 countries. The WAYPOINT trial tested tezepelumab in adults with severe CRSwNP. Both were randomised trials in people with severe disease, and the authors include drug-company employees.

These drugs are for severe, uncontrolled disease in adults. They are not a first step, and a specialist decides who qualifies.

## Can nasal polyps go away on their own?

**Polyps rarely disappear without treatment. Medicines can shrink them, and surgery can remove them, but they can return because the underlying inflammation remains.**

This is one reason the research above looks at whether biologics reduce the need for repeat surgery. Ongoing follow-up with an ear, nose and throat specialist is common.

## When should you see a doctor about nasal polyps?

**See a doctor if a blocked nose or lost smell lasts more than 12 weeks, or sooner if symptoms are one-sided or severe.**

Book a visit for:

- Stuffy nose, drip or facial pressure lasting 12 weeks or more
- Smell loss that does not return, especially if it came on suddenly
- Blockage on only one side, or a growth that bleeds
- Frequent nosebleeds along with blockage
- Snoring or poor sleep linked to a blocked nose

Get urgent care for swelling or redness around an eye, changes in vision or double vision, a severe headache, a stiff neck, confusion, or a high fever with nasal symptoms.
